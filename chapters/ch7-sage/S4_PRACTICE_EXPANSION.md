### *Somatic Markers: How to Know You're Actually Seeing*

The Sage has a body — even if the shadow Sage sometimes forgets that. The shadow Sage treats seeing as a mental exercise. The real Sage knows that the body is the instrument through which the view is lived.

**The body doesn't lie about which Sage you're in.**

When you're in genuine Sage sight — committed seeing, not detached narrating — you'll feel it in the body. The panoramic view feels like expansion. Not inflation. Not the chest full of air that precedes a boast. Expansion — the sense that you are taking in more without contracting around any of it. The body is upright, open, interested. The jaw is relaxed. The breathing is easy — not because there's no charge in the room, but because you've made room for the charge without being consumed by it.

When you're in shadow Sage — perspective as avoidance — the body will tell you. Closed. Pulled back. Observing from a distance that feels like safety but is actually isolation. The body is there but not *in* the room. The breathing is shallow — chest only, not the belly, not the ground. The eyes are looking at the room the way a tourist looks at a museum: everything is interesting and nothing is yours.

The return to the body is how you know the difference. Before you trust your view, check your body. Are you open or closed? Present or watching yourself from above? In the room or above it?

### *The Five Modes of Sight — Expanded Practice*

The Sage sees in five modes. Each one is a tool. Each one, used wrongly, becomes a shadow. Here's how each mode actually works in practice — not as description but as doing.

---

**1. The Panoramic Seer — Practice**

The capacity to see all altitudes at once, even briefly, is a moment of suspension. It requires that you pause — not in the room but before the room gets its grip on you.

Here's the practice: when you walk into a room or a conversation, before you respond — before you agree, disagree, strategize, or emote — take one breath and name the altitude you're walking into. Not solve it. Not fix it. Just name it. *This is a Red room. There's charge here. Someone's line got crossed.* Or: *This is an Orange room. We're in systems-thinking mode. We're looking for the lever.* Or: *This is a Green room. We need everyone to feel heard before anything else moves.*

Name it. Let the naming create a half-second of distance. That distance is the beginning of the Sage's freedom.

The shadow version: you name the altitude and then feel superior about knowing it. The Panoramic Seer becomes the narrator, and the narrating is a way to avoid being in the room. The fix is simple: after you name it, stay present. Don't pull back. Let the view inform what you do next without letting it replace what you do next.

---

**2. The Altitude-Switcher — Practice**

Once you can see all the altitudes, you can choose which one to operate from. The switch: deliberately moving up for context, or down for action.

The practice: after naming the altitude, ask: *Is this the altitude this moment actually requires? Or am I playing the altitude I default to because it's comfortable?*

Most people have a home altitude. The Challenger defaults to Red. The Architect defaults to Orange. The Diplomat defaults to Green. The home altitude is comfortable. It also means you treat every room like your home. That's useful when your home is what the room needs. It's a problem when the room needs something else.

The switch is a choice, not a default: *This situation is asking for Red, and I've been treating it like Green. Let me go to Red. Let me draw the line.* Or: *This situation is asking for Green, and I've been treating it like Red. Let me step back and hold the field.*

The switch requires you to be able to go down — to Red, to Orange, to Green — and still feel like yourself. Not be diminished by it. Not feel like you're simplifying. The Sage can hold Teal inside while operating at Red, without losing the Teal view. The shadow Sage treats the switch as decline. The real Sage knows the switch is the practice.

Here's the test: after switching altitudes, do you feel diminished or do you feel precise? Diminished is the shadow. Precise is the practice.

---

**3. The Diagnostician — Practice**

The Diagnostician is the Sage's most useful social tool. The capacity to name, out loud, which altitude a group is stuck at — and what that stuckness is costing them.

The practice: in a meeting, a conflict, a conversation that's going sideways, you say: *Here's what I've noticed. We're arguing about the strategy, but this is actually a boundary issue. We're treating a Red problem like an Orange one. That's why we're deadlocked — we keep looking for the right system when what we need is someone to draw a line.*

This is an act of service. It's also an act of risk. Because naming the altitude you're in is only comfortable if you're in Teal. If you're in Red and someone names it, Red feels seen — and being seen, in Red, can feel like being threatened. The Red part of you will push back. The Orange part will try to reframe. The Green part will want everyone to be nice about it.

The Sage names the altitude anyway. Not to be right. To be useful. Not from Teal as a height — from Teal as the capacity to hold all the altitudes and choose which one to speak from.

The shadow version: the naming becomes lecturing. The Sage who names everything from Teal becomes the shadow Sage who narrates-at-people, explaining the altitude of the room as if people in the room haven't already felt it. *I see you're at Red* is not the same as *I see you're at Red and I have some thoughts about what that means for all of us.* The first is居高临下. The second is service. The practice is the second.

Here's the test: after you name the altitude, do people feel seen or do they feel condescended to? Seen is the practice. Condescended is the shadow.

---

**4. The Liberator — Practice**

The Liberator is the Sage's most compassionate act: putting a game down when it's no longer serving.

The practice: ask, regularly — not in crisis but in reflection: *Is this game still mine? Am I still playing it because it matters, or because I've always played it? Is this the fight I was made for, or is it a fight I inherited from someone who needed it more than I do?*

Chou, in *10,000 Hours of Play*, writes about the importance of knowing when you're in the right game. The right game feels like play — like something you would do even if no one was watching, even if there was no reward, even if it cost you something. The wrong game feels like obligation. Like identity. Like *I am the person who fights for this, and if I stop fighting, I don't know who I am.*

The Liberator asks you to let go of the identity in order to keep the freedom. Not to stop caring about what you cared about. To stop caring about it as identity — to care about it as choice. Because the thing you care about is still true even when you're not the person who fights for it. The truth doesn't need you to be its champion. It just needs someone to tell the truth.

Here's the test: if you put this game down tomorrow, would the world be worse or would it be fine without you in that particular role? If the world would be fine — the game was about your identity, not the truth of it. If the world would actually be worse — then the game is still yours. Carry it deliberately.

---

**5. The Returner — Practice**

The Returner is the Sage's final move — and the one most often missed. After seeing, switching, diagnosing, and releasing — the Returner comes back to lower altitudes without condescension.

The practice: after operating at Teal — when you've seen the whole map and chosen your altitude and done the work — the Returner comes back down to where the village is. Not from above. Not with a sigh. Not with *you don't understand the real picture.* But with: *I'm back. I was up there for a minute. Here's where I've landed. And I'm staying here with you.*

This is the hardest mode to practice and the most often skipped. The Sage who can see everything but won't come back is not a Sage — they're a spectator with good vision. The real Sage goes to Teal, does the work, and comes back. Landed. Embodied. Present.

The shadow version: returning becomes retreating. The Sage who can't stay at Teal — who finds the responsibility of the view too heavy — uses the Return as escape. *I was too serious up there. Let me come back down to where things are simpler.* That's not the Returner. That's the Sage who went up too fast and hasn't done the work of being able to hold the altitude while staying in the body.

Here's the test: when you return, do you feel like you're coming home or like you're giving something up? Coming home is the practice. Giving something up is the shadow.

### *The Stage Sequence: See → Switch → Serve → Release → Return — A Working Example*

Here's how the sequence actually runs — not as theory but as a lived minute:

You walk into a meeting. Before anyone speaks, the Panoramic Seer takes one breath and names the room: *Red room. There's a boundary issue someone's trying to avoid.* You note it. You don't act on it yet.

The conversation starts. Someone makes an Orange move — proposes a system, looks for the process fix. The Altitude-Switcher checks: *Is this asking for Orange? Yes. Is this actually an Orange problem? No — the problem is Red but we're treating it like Orange.* You switch. You go to Red — you name the line, you name what's actually at stake. You do the work that Red calls for.

The Diagnostician is running the whole time. You notice when the room shifts from Red back to Green — when the boundary is named and now everyone needs to feel heard. You adjust. You meet them at Green without losing the Red you just named.

Now the field is clear. The Liberator asks: *Is this game mine? Am I the one who needs to hold this boundary, or did I just name it so someone else could?* You release — you let the boundary belong to the room, not just to you.

Then the Returner: you come back down. You sit with the result. You don't stay at Teal looking down. You come back to where they are. You say: *Here's where I landed. And I'm staying.*

The full loop takes a moment or a year. The sequence is the same. See. Switch. Serve. Release. Return.
