# CHAPTER 7: THE SAGE
## *Mastery Is Knowing Which Game You're Playing and Being Able to Put It Down*

---

## A Note Before the Exile

The first course I built for this work came out of a failure I did not understand yet.

George Floyd was murdered in May 2020. Within a week my phone had more messages in it than I had gotten in the previous year. Most of them were from white friends. For many of them, I was the only Black person they knew. I had been doing this work informally for years, coaching people through the emotional labor of showing up differently in their relationships, their workplaces, their lives. I had tools. I repackaged them into a course.

The premise was simple: allyship is just applied friendship and skilled helping. If I could make the work feel alive, people would do it. How hard could that be?

The course had less than a ten percent completion rate.

I did what I always do when something does not work: I doubled down. I made more content. I pushed harder. I tried to make the course better by making it bigger. Mid-course feedback told me it was not fun, which meant I had to build a section about how to find the source of fun inside a course I had promised would be fun. That is how far off I had drifted.

At the bottom of that failure I finally saw what was happening. I had taken responsibility for an experience that was not mine to control. I had started trying to drag people to water and then blame them for not drinking. I was right, and I was ineffective. That is a hard thing to admit when being right has been part of the job description.

The useful thing I learned was not that I should care less. It was that caring without placement is just force with better intentions.

That is why this chapter exists.

The thesis is simple: the Sage is not the person who sees the farthest, but the person who can see the whole board without turning that view into a hiding place. This matters when allyship becomes performance, superiority, or escape. If someone is in immediate danger, act. If a room needs a line, draw the line. Inner work is not a delay tactic; it is what keeps your action clean enough to keep using tomorrow.

The voices that came before this one prepared you for that move.

The Shaman taught you to feel the room before you explain it. The Challenger taught you to draw the line. The Regent taught you to carry forward what matters. The Architect taught you to make the right thing easier. The Diplomat taught you to stay with people who are not in the same game you are in. The Sage is what happens when those voices stop competing and start working together.

That is also where the Effective Allyship Formula shows up in the chapter:

1. identify your superpower
2. identify who needs that superpower
3. enroll allies to help you help those people
4. show up consistently

The Sage keeps that formula honest. It helps you see when your superpower is becoming performance, when your consistency is really just staying in the wrong game, and when the room needs a different move than the one you are proudest of.

## Section 1: The Exile and the Gift

There was a time when the Sage lived inside the village's conversations.

The Sage could sit in a room and see all the games at once. The Sage could feel when a strategy conversation was actually a boundary problem. The Sage could hear when a harmony conversation was hiding a power struggle. The Sage could notice when a room shifted from one game to another and nobody else had caught it yet.

That was useful at first. The Sage could name what the room was really doing. The room would pause. Sometimes the pause was enough.

Then something changed.

The village heard seeing as leaving.

If you could see the pattern of the game, the village decided, then you must not really be in it. Wisdom started to mean distance. Perspective started to mean opting out. The person who saw everything became the person who was no longer supposed to belong.

That was the exile.

Not a dramatic banishment. A quieter one. The room simply stopped receiving what the Sage was saying. And when that happened enough times, the Sage learned to get quiet.

That quiet is one of the first things that can go wrong in allyship.

You know the room I mean. The meeting where the stated agenda is strategy but the real agenda is who gets to win. The circle where one person is using the group process to stay at the center. The conversation where someone is drawing a line and the line is correct, but the way it is drawn has more to do with being right than with protecting anyone.

In those rooms, you can probably feel what is actually happening underneath what is being said.

And then, sometimes, you stop saying it.

Not because you are wrong. Because you learned the cost of being right in a room that does not know what to do with clarity.

That is how exile becomes a habit.

The gift does not disappear. It just goes underground until you learn how to use it without turning it into distance.

## Section 2: The Distortion

Once the Sage goes quiet, the village does not stop playing games.

It just stops knowing which games it is playing.

Every conflict starts to feel existential. A boundary problem looks like an identity crisis. A systems problem looks like a values problem. People keep moving, but motion starts to get mistaken for progress.

That is how Sage becomes distortion.

Not the real Sage. The version of Sage that learns to name everything without ever having to choose. The version that can describe the board without ever having to move a piece. Perspective becomes a place to stand above the room instead of a way to serve it.

Here are the most common ways that looks in allyship:

**Trauma Olympics.** Pain becomes a credential. The Sage in distortion can validate everyone and still miss that somebody is losing. When pain becomes status, unprocessed hurt gets rewarded and healed hurt gets ignored.

**Captain Save-a-Kid.** The helper solves the problem they find most interesting, not the one in front of them. The wrong problem gets a beautiful answer. The person who needed a line gets a system. The person who needed to be heard gets a framework.

**Dragging horses to water.** The design is elegant, but the people inside it are not the people the design assumes. The Sage in distortion keeps trying to fix the people instead of checking whether the room is actually asking for that kind of move.

These are all good intentions with the wrong game underneath them.

That is the real danger here. The Sage in distortion is often helpful. The Sage in distortion is often intelligent. The Sage in distortion is often right.

But the rightness does not help if it is happening from above the room instead of inside it.

So the question becomes: how do you know when you are actually seeing?

The answer is simple: does the sight help the room move, or does it help you stand above it?

## Section 3: The Tool

The Sage's job is not to see more than everybody else.

The Sage's job is to know which game is actually being played and to choose a move that fits that game.

That is what this chapter gives you:

**See → Switch → Serve → Release → Return**

### See

First, name the game.

What is actually happening here? Is this a power game, a strategy game, a harmony game, or a whole-board view moment? If you do not know what game the room is in, you will keep offering the wrong thing with great confidence.

Seeing is not distance. Seeing is orientation.

### Switch

Once you know the game, choose the right one.

If the room is in the power game, do not answer it with a strategy memo. If the room is in the harmony game, do not answer it with a line that only works if people are already ready for conflict. Switch deliberately.

This is not inconsistency. This is literacy.

### Serve

Then do the work that game actually requires.

If the room needs a line, draw the line. If the room needs a bridge, build the bridge. If the room needs a clearer system, design the system. The move only counts if it helps the actual room, not the version of the room you wish existed.

### Release

When the game is done, put it down.

Not every game is yours forever. Some games are for a season, and some were only ever meant to teach you something and then end. Releasing is not giving up. Releasing is making room for the next right move.

### Return

Come back to the room without condescension.

This is the hardest part. You saw the whole board, but the room may not have. Return as someone who is with the people in the room, not above them. Stay in the room. Stay kind. Let them not understand, and let that be okay.

That sequence is the practical heart of this chapter.

It is also the practical heart of the Effective Allyship Formula:

- use your superpower in the right room
- do not use it to prove yourself
- enroll allies who can help you keep going
- stay consistent enough for the work to matter

## Section 4: Examples

Here is what the tool looks like in real life.

You walk into a meeting. Before anyone says much, you can feel that the room is not really talking about strategy. It is talking about who gets to decide what matters. You name it to yourself first. That is See.

Someone starts pushing a systems fix. The room actually needs a boundary named. You switch. That is Switch.

You draw the line, calmly and clearly. You do the thing the moment requires. That is Serve.

Once the line has done its work, you do not keep holding the room hostage with your insight. You let the move land. That is Release.

Then you stay in the room. You do not disappear into the high view. You come back into the conversation with the people who are still working through what happened. That is Return.

Or maybe the room is in the harmony game. People need to feel heard before anything else can happen. If you come in with your best line too early, you lose the room. So you switch. You hold the field. You slow down. You meet people where they are.

The same tool works in different allyship situations:

- when you are overexplaining
- when you are rescuing
- when you are too good at watching from above
- when you need to leave a game you have outgrown
- when you need to come back down and stay present

That is what makes the Sage useful. Not the altitude by itself. The altitude used in service of the room.

## Section 5: Practice and Formula Check

Try this with one real situation.

1. Name the game you are in.
2. Name your superpower.
3. Notice where you are tempted to use it as proof instead of service.
4. Identify who actually needs that power.
5. Switch deliberately.
6. Serve the room.
7. Enroll the allies who can help you keep going.
8. Release the game when it is done.
9. Return without making your clarity into a performance.

If you want a simpler version, use this:

- What game is this?
- What does the room actually need?
- What move will help?
- Which step of the Effective Allyship Formula is active here?

That is enough.

The point is not to become a perfect Sage. The point is to stop using perspective as a hiding place.

---

## When Seeing Becomes Stalling

The Sage's characteristic failure is not a bad read — it is a true read used to avoid the move. So name the trap directly: inner work is not a delay tactic.

If someone is in immediate danger, act. If the room needs a line, draw it. The whole-board view earns its keep only when it ends in a move. The moment "I'm still seeing it clearly" becomes the reason you never serve, you have turned perspective into the most sophisticated hiding place there is. The test is time-bound: if you have seen the game and still not moved, you are no longer seeing — you are stalling. Switch. Serve. The sight was always for the sake of the move.

---

## Section 6: Summary and Transition

The Sage gives you perspective with commitment.

That means:

- you can see the whole board
- you can choose the right game
- you can put games down when they are done
- you can come back into the room without losing what you saw

What the Sage does not give you is permission to stay above the work.

The real test of Sage is not whether you can name the game. It is whether you can name it, move in it, and then come back down and still be in the room when you land.

That is the gift this chapter offers the book: the capacity to see the game underneath allyship without turning that sight into another form of distance.

And once you can do that, the next question changes.

It is no longer, "Which Face am I being?"

It becomes, "What am I building now?"

That is the Player.

The chapter ends here on purpose: see clearly, move cleanly, come back down.
