# CHAPTER 1: THE SHAMAN
## *Allyship Is Relational, Consent-Based, and Trust-Earned*

---

## Section 1: The Promise

Here is the version of allyship that keeps failing:

You see a problem. You respond. You show up, you post, you speak, you push. Three months later the problem is still there, or it has transformed into something your original response can't touch. And you are exhausted. Not because you did the wrong things, but because the premise was wrong from the beginning.

The premise was: allyship is mainly about what you do in public.

The deeper truth is this: allyship is relational. It happens between people, through relationship, with consent, over time. You can support a movement. You ally with people. Those are not the same gesture, and confusing them is where most of the burnout lives.

This chapter gives you one thing: a definition that holds.

**Allyship is the art of increasing well-being, agency, and safety — in the form that matters here — with consent, through relationship.**

Everything in this book hangs on that sentence. Especially the phrase *with consent.* Because the single most common allyship failure is not cruelty or indifference. It is showing up with help nobody asked for, aimed at a form of well-being the other person does not share, delivered with the confidence of someone who knows what's needed.

That is not allyship. That is rescue.

And rescue, however loving, removes agency from the person you are trying to serve.

The Effective Allyship Formula runs through all eight chapters. Here it is once, so you have the spine:

1. identify your superpower
2. identify who needs it
3. enroll allies to help
4. show up consistently

This chapter establishes the ground the formula stands on: **consent and trust are not courtesies. They are the mechanism.** Without them, the formula produces skilled rescue, not allyship. With them, it produces something that can actually hold.

---

## Section 2: The Diagnosis — What Gets in the Way

The reason allyship keeps failing is not that people don't care. People who pick up this book care enormously.

The problem is a bad theory of change.

The old theory goes: *learn, feel, act.* It is not wrong. It is incomplete. The old allyship taught you what to do. It did not teach you how to stay in relationship while you do it. How to ask instead of assume. How to earn trust before you leverage it. How to recognize when the help you are offering is for you, not for the person in front of you.

The origin of this book is a rock in my shoe.

George Floyd was murdered in May of 2020. My phone filled up — white friends, mostly, checking in, asking if I was okay. And I was glad they called. The reaching out mattered.

But something else was also there.

A low friction, persistent, the way a stone in your shoe is not agony but is also not nothing. I followed it. What I found was rage. Not grief. Rage.

Here is the part I did not say out loud for a long time: the rage was not new. I had learned, for reasons that started early and went deep, to keep my feelings below the waterline — away from other people's awareness, and often away from my own. The calls were supposed to support me. Most of them were delivering unprocessed feelings to me, gift-wrapped as concern. I was the only Black person most of those people knew. My well-being had become a stand-in for something much bigger, without my consent.

I was not okay. But not for the reasons they assumed.

That is a specific allyship failure. The intent was genuine. The consent was absent. The form of care on offer was the form the helper needed to give, not the form I needed to receive.

**The distortion at this altitude is this:** you mistake the impulse to help for an invitation to help. They are not the same. The impulse lives in you. The invitation has to come from the other person.

When the impulse runs the show without the invitation, even skilled helping produces harm — not because the helper is cruel, but because the helper has made themselves the author of someone else's story. Well-being that someone else defines for you is not well-being. It is management.

This is what makes the consent principle non-negotiable.

## Section 3: The Tool — The Allyship Definition and Invitation Language

The one tool this chapter gives you is a definition and the language that follows from it.

**The definition:**

> Allyship is the art of increasing well-being, agency, and safety — in the form that matters here — with consent, through relationship. You ally with people; you support movements.

Let's break down what each phrase is doing.

**"increasing well-being, agency, and safety"** — this is the direction. Not just feeling better. Being more capable, more free to move, more protected from harm. All three. The goal is not dependence; it is capacity.

**"in the form that matters here"** — well-being is not universal. The Shaman's version of well-being — emotional truth, felt safety, mutual trust — is not the same as the Architect's version (leverage, clear systems, the right thing made easier). Each chapter in this book defines the kind of well-being that belongs at that altitude. This phrase keeps the ally from imposing their preferred flavor of help.

**"with consent"** — this is the mechanism, not the nicety. Consent is not a check-box at the start. It is an ongoing orienting question: *does the person I am helping want this help, in this form, at this moment?* Consent can be withdrawn. Consent can evolve. The ally tracks it.

**"through relationship"** — this means over time, with trust as the currency. You do not build trust by knowing the right things. You build it by showing up consistently, by being reliable enough that the other person can predict what they will get from you, and by staying after the moment is over.

**"You ally with people; you support movements"** — a movement is a current. You can stand in it, amplify it, give it resources, stay aligned with its direction. But you cannot be in relationship with a movement the way you can be in relationship with a person. Confusing these produces performative allyship: gestures aimed at a cause that bypass the person in front of you.

---

**The invitation language — how to ask and how to earn**

Because consent is the mechanism, the first skill is knowing how to ask for it and how to receive the answer.

Here is what asking sounds like in practice:

*"What would actually be useful to you here?"*  
*"I have some capacity — is there something you need, and is it something I can offer?"*  
*"Before I make any move, I want to make sure I'm helping with what matters to you, not what I'm good at."*

These are not scripts. They are orientation questions. Their purpose is to move the help off of your assumptions and back onto the other person's actual situation.

Here is what earning trust sounds like over time:

Trust is built by doing the small thing you said you would do. Repeatedly. Without needing the other person to celebrate it. Without making your reliability into a performance.

The ally who earns trust is not the one who shows up loudest in the crisis. It is the one who showed up the week before the crisis, and the week before that, without waiting to be needed.

Consent-seeking and trust-building are not preliminary steps before the real work begins. They are the work. Everything else in the formula — finding your superpower, identifying who needs it, enrolling other allies, staying consistent — depends on this ground.

## Section 4: What This Looks Like in Real Situations

**The phone call in 2020.**

My friends called with care. They had not asked what I needed. What I needed was not to be the designated Black person absorbing their feelings about a Black man's murder. The gap between their intent and what the situation actually called for is the same gap that produces most allyship failures. Intent is about the helper. Impact is about the person being helped. Allyship lives in the impact zone.

What consent would have looked like there: *"I want to reach out. Is this a good moment? What, if anything, would actually be helpful?"* And then waiting for the answer, and accepting it — including the answer that says *nothing right now.*

**The colleague who is struggling.**

You see a colleague who has clearly had a hard week. The old move is to offer your assessment of what they need — a pep talk, a resource, a referral, a plan. The new move is simpler and harder: ask. *"I see you're carrying something. Do you want company, a problem solved, or for me to just leave it alone?"*

Those are three different forms of care. Offering the wrong one, however generously, closes the person down instead of opening them up. The right one — even if it's *leave it alone* — communicates that their agency matters more to you than your helpfulness.

**The meeting where you have power and they don't.**

You are in a room where the other person has less structural power than you. You want to use your position to help them. Here, consent does a specific piece of work: it protects against the version of allyship where the helper's power over the situation becomes another source of the other person's powerlessness.

*"Here is something I could do. Would that be useful? Would you want it done differently? Is there a reason not to do this I can't see from where I'm sitting?"*

That sequence does not weaken the help. It sharpens it, because it corrects for the blind spots that come with structural advantage.

**The moment when someone says no.**

This is where allyship is actually tested. Consent includes the right to refuse help. The ally who can receive *no* without making the other person manage their disappointment — without sulking, without withdrawing, without making the refusal into a commentary on their own goodness — is the ally who gets trusted enough to be asked for help later.

The no is not a rejection of you. It is information about what the person actually needs. Follow it.

## Section 5: Practice — The One Conversation

Think of one person in your life toward whom you feel some form of allyship pull — someone you want to support, protect, or help in some way.

Now ask yourself these three questions, in order:

1. **Have I asked this person what they actually need?** Not assumed. Not inferred from what I would need. Asked.

2. **Do they know I am willing to help?** Not because I announced it, but because I have shown up reliably enough that they can assume it.

3. **Is there anything I have been offering that they have not asked for?**

The third question is the uncomfortable one. Most of us have at least one relationship where we are working harder to help than the other person has invited. The work often feels loving. To the other person, it can feel like pressure, assessment, or the implication that they are not managing their own life well enough.

If the answer to question three is yes: stop. Not because you were wrong to care, but because care that isn't wanted is not care anymore.

The practice this week is one conversation. Find the person. Ask what they actually need. Accept the answer.

That is the whole exercise. It is harder than it sounds, and it is also the most direct way to find out whether what you have been calling allyship is relationship or rescue.

## But What If I Just Do Nothing, Then?

The objection to consent-first allyship: doesn't waiting for an invitation mean standing by while harm happens?

Consent is the mechanism for *help*, not a veto on *action.* When someone is in danger, you act — you do not poll the room first. The consent principle governs the gray zone where most allyship actually lives: the colleague having a hard week, the friend in grief, the person whose situation you have decided you already understand. There, asking is not passivity. It is the difference between help and imposition. And "I asked, and they said no" is not doing nothing — it is doing the harder thing, which is letting someone keep authorship of their own life.

## Section 6: Summary and Handoff

Allyship is relational, consent-based, and trust-earned.

You ally with people. You support movements. They are not the same gesture.

The well-being that matters at this altitude is mutual: safety, consent, and trust between both people, not care flowing in one direction without the other person's say.

The Effective Allyship Formula begins here, on this ground. Every subsequent step — identifying your superpower, finding who needs it, enrolling help, staying consistent — runs on the trust and consent you build now. Without this ground, the formula produces skilled effort that bypasses the person it is meant to serve.

**What to carry forward:**

- Allyship requires an invitation, not just an impulse.
- Consent is not a formality. It is the mechanism that keeps help from becoming control.
- Trust is built by reliability over time, not by the size of your gesture in the moment.
- The definition: *increasing well-being, agency, and safety — in the form that matters here — with consent, through relationship.*

**The handoff:**

Now that you have the ground allyship stands on, the next question is: what do you bring to it?

Allyship without a clear sense of what you actually carry — your real capacities, your genuine gifts, the specific things you can offer that are not performances of what a good ally is supposed to look like — becomes a posture. Effective allyship starts with knowing your superpower.

That is Chapter 2: let's find where yours comes from.
