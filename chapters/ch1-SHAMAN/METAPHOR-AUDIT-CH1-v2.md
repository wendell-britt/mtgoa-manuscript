## 2. The Structural Problem: Shaman Chapter Has No Water

Lakoff's framework says: **metaphor is not decoration — it structures thought.** The Shaman is the Water face. Water is its primary EA channel. Without water metaphor, the Shaman's signature somatic experience — grief as depth, feeling-into, the body as vessel that holds and transforms — has no metaphorical structure to land in.

The chapter has 13 explicit mentions of "grief" but almost none of them are grounded in water metaphor. Grief is described, named, located, and offered back to the reader. But it is never *felt as water*. The difference:

- **Water metaphor present:** "Your grief is a lake. You have been swimming in it for years, and you didn't know it was yours." — This would let the reader feel grief as a container, a depth, a place they've been swimming.
- **What the chapter actually does:** "Grief is not weakness. It is the cost of having loved something." — True, but abstract. The reader thinks about grief, doesn't feel it.

This is the inverse of the Challenger problem. The Challenger had too much fire (24 mentions) and needed to be grounded in boundary/line somatic language. The Shaman has almost no water and needs to be grounded in water metaphor to make grief feel like water.

## 6. The Specific Gap: WATER Metaphor Not Applied

The chapter claims to introduce the Shaman (the water face) and the 5 emotional channels (which include Water/Sadness). But the primary metaphor for the Water channel — **FEELING IS DEPTH / GRIEF IS WATER** — is almost entirely absent.

This is a consequence of the 8 Gates structure. Gates are spatial containers — you pass through them, you stand at them, you answer them. Gates are vertically oriented (in/out) and categorically discrete (this gate or that gate). They are not water metaphors. The 8 Gates map in S6 is structurally incompatible with a water metaphor — the reader can't be swimming and passing through a gate simultaneously.

**This means the chapter has two competing frameworks:**
1. The 8 Gates — a discrete, vertical, container metaphor (correct for the Regent's inheritance framework)
2. The Shaman/Water channel — a depth, flow, saturation metaphor (correct for grief-as-feeling-into)

The 8 Gates won the structural battle in this draft. The water metaphor lost. The result is a Shaman chapter with almost no water.

## 8. Editorial Recommendations

**For this draft (Ch1 as threshold):**
1. Add one somatic water metaphor to S7 Journey to the Center — a place where the reader is invited to feel grief as depth, not just name it. One paragraph of water metaphor would close this gap without changing the chapter's structure.
2. Consider adding a water anchor to the emotional channels table (S5 or S9) — e.g., "Sadness is the water channel: what you feel when you've been swimming in something too long."

**For the larger editorial framework:**
3. Lakoff's principle — **metaphor structures thought** — means the 8 Gates structure and the Water/Shaman channel are in genuine tension. A reader who learns the 8 Gates framework will experience grief through a gate-lens (discrete, passable, answerable) rather than a water-lens (deep, saturating, felt-into). This is not a problem in Ch1 because Ch1 is a threshold chapter. But Ch2 (the full Shaman) needs to undo the gate-lens before it can introduce the water-lens.
4. The framework should note: **Gates metaphors and water metaphors are not interchangeable.** They produce different reader experiences. The manuscript's editorial process should track which metaphor family each chapter uses and ensure the transition chapters (Ch1, Ch5) don't lock in the wrong metaphor for what comes next.

## 9. Dogfood: Applying Lakoff to MTGOA's Own Process

The audit finds that **the editorial process can introduce new metaphors without removing the old ones** — the +25 body words were added without deleting the existing line/hold vocabulary. This is Lakoff's "highlighting and hiding" principle in action: the editorial pass highlighted body vocabulary while leaving existing metaphors intact.

But it also finds that **new structural frameworks (like the 8 Gates) can override existing metaphorical families** (like water) simply by occupying the largest section of the chapter (50%). Metaphor is not just word choice — it is what gets the most space, the most development, the most structural weight.

This has implications for the editorial process:
- Track not just what metaphors are present, but which metaphors get the most chars/development
- When adding a new structural framework (like the 8 Gates), check whether it conflicts with the intended metaphorical family of the chapter's primary face
- The "practice section concentration" pattern (S4/S5 with 22-28% of chapter) may need to be adjusted for threshold chapters — Ch1's S6 at 50% is structurally appropriate for an overview, but it means the water metaphor can't land in the way it needs to
