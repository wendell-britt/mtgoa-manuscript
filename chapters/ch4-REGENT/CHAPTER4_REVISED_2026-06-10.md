## Section 1: The Exile
### *Why the Regent Left the Village (And What the Village Lost)*

There was a time when the village tried to run on fire alone.

The Challenger had drawn lines, named costs, refused and demanded and named the violations. All of that worked — for a while. But fire burns out. And when the fire burned out, there was nothing underneath. The Challenger didn't stay. The lines were drawn and the Challenger moved on. That left the village with boundaries that existed in the moment but evaporated by next week.

This is when the Regent showed up.

The Regent arrived with something quieter and harder to see: *structure.* The Regent said: "You've drawn the lines. Now let me show you how to build the city around them." The Regent created the first traditions — the weekly council that opened with the village's stated values, the yearly ceremony that renewed the vows, the roles that passed from person to person so that when one person left, the role stayed and the next person could step into it. The Regent took the Challenger's fire and turned it into something that could last.

But the Regent made a mistake that cost everything.

The Regent started to confuse *what the village had built* with *what the village actually was.* The walls became more important than what they were protecting. The traditions became more important than the people they served. The Regent started to believe that the structure *was* the village — that if you kept the walls intact, everything inside them was fine.

And it wasn't fine. The people inside were disappearing. Councils went through the motions. Ceremonies nobody believed in. Roles filled by people who had long since stopped feeling anything. The Regent saw these as maintenance problems and tried to solve human disappearance with better structure.

The Architect saw it differently. "You're building a city inside a graveyard. The people are gone. You're maintaining their ghosts."

The Regent didn't take this well. The argument escalated. And when the dust settled, the Regent was gone — not exiled exactly, but retired. The village kept the walls. What it lost was the person who knew how to tend them — who could tell the difference between a tradition that still served and a tradition that was just a comfortable cage.

The village never meant for that to happen.

---

I came up in traditions. Martial arts, church, academia — all of them Regent organizations, all of them built on the assumption that what the person before you learned was worth receiving. You showed up, and the tradition showed up with you, and over time it became part of what you could offer.

I've spent most of my adult life in rooms where that's read as a threat.

There was a collective I was part of for a few years. Progressive, justice-focused, the right values on paper, and absolutely no governance. Not because they forgot — because structure was suspect. Roles meant hierarchy meant oppression. *We'll figure it out as we go* was a principle, not a gap.

I could feel it coming. The way you can feel a building that's going to fail — not because of the people in it but because there's no floor under them. I said things. Careful things, the kind you say in rooms allergic to structure. I tried to name what I was watching without naming it in the way they feared: the authoritarian, the traditionalist, the one who wants to run things. So I held back some of what I knew. I wore the crown under my hood.

The ones who wanted the crown were usually looking for someone to give themselves to. They weren't receiving the tradition — they were looking for a patriarch. Which is its own kind of problem.

The first crisis, they adapted. The second, they circled. By the third, they were arguing about the same things from different angles with no memory of what the original argument had been for.

I stayed. I helped pick up the pieces. That's what martial arts and the church and watching things dissolve for twenty years actually prepares you for. You hold what's worth holding. You help people sort out what happened.

But I've never figured out how to offer the floor before the crisis. The tradition is only legible to people who've already lost something. By then, the invitation to receive it has a very different shape than I wanted it to have.

## Section 2: The Distortion
### *What the Village Does With Tradition When the Regent Is Gone*

Here's what happened after the Regent left: the village didn't stop building.

It just stopped knowing *why.*

The village kept the walls. It kept the ceremonies. It kept the roles and the traditions and the weekly councils and the yearly renewals and all the structures the Regent had built. But without the Regent there to tend them — to ask, every time: *does this still serve the people inside it?* — the structures started to calcify.

The ceremonies became empty. The council meetings ran their agenda and nobody knew why they were actually meeting. The roles got filled by people going through motions, saying words they didn't believe, performing loyalty they didn't feel. And the traditions — the ones that used to carry the village's actual values — became cargo cults. The village did the thing the Regent taught them to do, but the meaning underneath it was gone.

The village didn't realize this was happening. The village thought it was *being faithful.* The Regent had said: *guard the tradition.* So the village guarded it. They guarded it hard. They held the line on how things were done. They enforced the roles. They kept the walls high. They said: *this is how we've always done it* and meant it as a defense — as if "how we've always done it" was the same thing as "what actually works."

It wasn't.

The distortion the village developed was specific and recognizable: **obedience without understanding.** The village learned to follow the Regent's structures without knowing *why* the structures existed. They kept the form and lost the function. They said the words and missed the meaning. They performed the role and forgot the person.

This is the Regent in distortion: the cage built out of love. The tradition that stops serving and starts controlling. The role that consumes the human instead of being carried by them.

The village, without the Regent, becomes a museum. Beautiful and empty and slowly falling apart.

---

You've been in organizations that said they were built on shared values and turned out to be built on whoever held the most power to define what "shared" meant.

You've been in spaces where "tradition" was how the people who came before you protected what they'd built — at the expense of the people who came after. Where "this is how we've always done it" was a sentence specifically designed to stop your questions from landing. Where the organization's stated purpose and its actual purpose had quietly separated, and everyone who pointed that out got managed out, burned out, or simply stopped caring.

You came out of those spaces with a principle: you weren't going to build something that way. You were going to build something fluid, horizontal, alive. You were going to let the thing evolve as the people evolved. Hold the structure lightly.

And then you watched what you built dissolve.

Not dramatically. Not one catastrophic failure. Slowly — the way a fire goes out when you stop feeding it. The founding energy burned through. The people who cared most burned out. The ones who stayed started having different ideas about what the thing was for, and there was no structure underneath the disagreement to hold it. Every decision returned to first principles because there were no second principles. Every new person had to be walked through the founding vision from scratch because there was no inheritance to hand them. The whole weight of the thing rested on whoever was most energized at any given moment — and when those people left, the thing went with them.

You know which part of that paragraph is yours. I don't need to name it.

Here's what this chapter is not: a defense of the organizations that used tradition to protect power. That happened. It kept happening. The thing you walked away from was real.

Here's what it is: a question about what you built instead, and whether it lasted, and whether you can see the connection.

The Regent's gift is not loyalty to the past. The Regent's gift is the ability to pass something down — to build a thing that is bigger than any one person's presence in it, that knows how to receive a new person and what to give them, that can survive the loss of its founder. That gift is not the same as conservatism. It is not the same as rigidity. It is not the same as "this is how we've always done it."

It is what makes the things you build last longer than you do.

## The Fixer-Healer Gate

The Fixer has been here longer than you know.

It's the part that looks at a broken thing and cannot rest until it's repaired. That moves toward rupture instead of away from it. That knows, intuitively, what needs to be held in place so everything else can function. The Fixer is not a passive part — it's one of the most active voices you carry. And like the Protector before it and the Controller before that, it developed this drive for a reason. The Fixer learned to repair because repair was necessary. The Fixer learned to hold because holding was what kept things from collapsing.

The Fixer has two jobs. Each one carries its own epiphany.

**The first job is repairing.** The Fixer sees the rupture and moves. This is a genuine gift — most people flee from broken things; the Fixer runs toward them. But here's what the Fixer rarely asks: *who defined this as broken, and whose standard of fixed am I working toward?* Most of us have been repairing the same ruptures for years — the same dynamics, the same patterns, the same failures — without asking whether the repair is working for the people in the room or for an authority in our heads. The Regent we inherited, not the one we chose.

Take a moment with that. What have you been repairing? Notice the list — the ruptures, the dynamics, the patterns. Now ask: *whose standard of fixed am I working toward?* If there's a face that appears — a leader, a framework, a movement's definition of what good looks like — that's your inherited Regent. That's the authority you've been working for, maybe without choosing it.

---

One thing to notice before the second job: the charge that sent you toward the first one. The feeling that arose right before you moved to repair — or that's been holding you in place.

That charge came from somewhere. It was the Controller handing the Fixer its orders.

You learned this in Chapter 2: the Controller sets the emotional standard. The Fixer is the hands of that standard. The Controller decides what counts as broken; the Fixer executes the repair. The Controller decides what the collapse would cost; the Fixer holds the thing in place. So the question "whose standard of fixed am I working toward?" isn't only about the authority figure in your head. It's about the charge activating the move.

I've spent time watching myself fix things and then feeling empty afterward. Not because the fix didn't work — sometimes it did. But the fix was running on obligation, and obligation is the Controller's non-renewable fuel. When I learned to check the charge first — to ask whether the impulse to repair was coming from genuine care or from "the standard demands this" — I started fixing fewer things and actually helping more. The charge is the signal. Read it the way Chapter 2 taught you.

---

**The second job is holding in place.** The Fixer also guards what shouldn't break. Keeps the structure stable. Protects what's worth protecting. This is also a genuine gift — the person who holds the thing together while everyone else panics is doing real work. But here's what the Fixer rarely asks: *is this still worth holding?* Some of what you're holding is genuinely worth protecting. Some of it is a tradition that should have changed years ago — and you've been holding it in place because you inherited the obligation rather than chose the loyalty.

Take a moment with that too. What have you been holding in place? And when you look at each thing — is it something you *chose* to protect, or something you were handed and never put down? Is the charge keeping you there loyalty that feels chosen, or loyalty the Controller won't let you release?

The two jobs together point at the same question: *who is the Regent you've been working for?*

Not accusation — inquiry. The Fixer's jobs are real and valuable. The question is just whether they're aimed at a Regent who still deserves them. And the charge that activates each job is the place to look.

- *What I have been repairing, and for whom*
- *What I have been holding in place, and whether it still serves*

Two lines. That's the gate's work. You don't have to resolve them in this chapter. You just have to see them.

## Section 3: The Concept
### *What the Regent's Practice Actually Is (And Why It's Not What You've Been Told)*

Before anything else: the Regent's practice is about *chosen* loyalty — not the loyalty that was handed to you, not the loyalty you perform because leaving would cost too much, not the loyalty that mistakes obligation for love. Chosen loyalty is the act of someone who has looked at what they are carrying, decided it is worth carrying, and picked it up again on purpose. Everything that follows in this chapter assumes that distinction. When it says loyalty, it means chosen loyalty. You'll know the difference in the body.

You already have a version of what "tradition" means. You've been carrying it since the last time someone used it against you.

*A client of mine spent three years in a martial arts school that had been running the same curriculum since the 1970s. The kata were beautiful. The transmission was real — forty years of people learning to fall correctly so they didn't get hurt in the street. She stayed because something about the continuity was worth staying for.*

*But the school also had a rule about how women were trained that had been designed in 1974 and never updated. She knew it was wrong. Every instructor in the building probably knew it was wrong. Nobody changed it because changing it felt like breaking the lineage. Like the tradition would crack if you pulled that thread.*

*That's what inherited loyalty looks like from the inside. It doesn't feel like cowardice. It feels like faithfulness. The rule stays not because anyone defends it on the merits but because nobody wants to be the one who broke what their teachers gave them.*

*What she eventually learned — and it took longer than she'd like to admit — is that changing the rule was the more faithful act. The lineage was about people learning to protect themselves. The 1974 rule was undermining exactly that. Chosen loyalty asked: what is this tradition actually trying to do? And then: is the current form still doing it? Inherited loyalty said: this is how we've always done it. Those are not the same question.*

It's not nostalgia. That's the village version — the version that says "we do it this way because we've always done it this way." That version treats tradition as a *relic* — something preserved because it's old, not because it works. That's not the Regent's practice. That's a cargo cult of the Regent's practice.

The Regent's actual practice is something harder and more alive: **the deliberate passing down of what works, across time, so that the next person doesn't have to figure it out from scratch.**

That's inheritance as a living act. Not preserving a fossil — building a lineage. Not keeping the past alive — *preparing* the future with the lessons you learned.

Here's what this means in practice:

**A tradition is not a rule. A tradition is a *gift.*** The person who came before you learned something through costly experience — through failure, through grief, through years of working something out. Rather than making you learn it the same way, they encoded what they learned into a form you could receive. A ceremony. A role. A practice. A language. A set of rituals that carry the teaching inside them.

When you participate in a living tradition, you're not submitting to an authority. You're *receiving a gift from someone who paid for it so you wouldn't have to.*

That's what the Regent knows. That's what the Regent built. The weekly council isn't a bureaucratic obligation — it's a gift from every person who showed up to that council for the last thirty years and used it to work something out, so that when you show up, you have a structure to work in. The roles aren't cages — they're inheritance. Each person who fills the role adds something to it and passes it to the next person. The tradition *grows.*

And here's the part that makes the Regent's practice distinct: the Regent knows that traditions are *supposed* to change. A tradition that can't change is a tradition that has died — it's just still moving because nobody noticed. The Regent's actual practice is: *receive what was passed to you, honor what still serves, reform what no longer does, and pass something worth passing.*

That's the full sequence: **Inherit → Honor → Steward → Reform → Entrust.**

Not: obey.
Not: preserve.
Not: protect at all costs.

**Inherit. Honor. Steward. Reform. Entrust.**

The Regent's mistake — the distortion that exiled them — was confusing stewardship with preservation. The Regent started to think that protecting the tradition meant keeping it exactly as it was received. But a tradition kept exactly as received is a snapshot of someone else's moment, not a living thing that can serve the next person's moment.

Real stewardship includes the possibility of reform. Real inheritance includes the possibility that what you received needs to change before you pass it on.

## Section 4: The Practice
### *The Cycle of Inheritance — How Loyalty Actually Works*

Here's the question the village never taught you to ask: what did you inherit?

Not your genetics. Not your culture in the abstract. I'm talking about the specific bundle of practices, wounds, loyalties, and assumptions that arrived in your life before you had any say in the matter — and that you've been carrying ever since, sometimes knowing why, often not.

Every person alive is an inheritor. The Regent's practice is about working consciously with that fact instead of against it.

The native material at the Regent's altitude is not emotion. It's not will. It's not even logic. It's **loyalty** — the felt sense of belonging to something larger than yourself, and the weight that comes with that belonging. What you owe. What you carry. What you pass on.

Loyalty is deeply unfashionable in progressive circles. It sounds like obedience. It sounds like the thing that keeps people inside harmful institutions. But that's because the village conflated loyalty with compliance — and then wondered why the institutions it served became so brittle.

Real loyalty is chosen. Real loyalty sees the whole inheritance — the gift and the damage — and decides, with full knowledge, to carry it forward. Real loyalty can be broken when the tradition breaks you. Real loyalty can be reformed when the tradition no longer serves what it originally served. That's not disloyalty. That's the most loyal thing there is.

This section teaches the Regent's cycle: how to work with your inheritance consciously, move through it cleanly, and pass it forward without passing on the wounds.

---

### The 5 Channels — How Loyalty Shows Up

The Regent works with loyalty in five distinct modes. Each one is a different relationship to what was passed down.

**1. The Custodian** — The mode of caring for what exists. Not changing it. Not judging it. Just noticing: this is here. This has lasted. Someone tended this before me. *What have I been given that still works?*

**2. The Inheritor** — The mode of receiving — including receiving the damage. The Inheritor knows that every inheritance carries both medicine and wound. *What came with my inheritance that I didn't choose?*

**3. The Teacher** — The mode of passing it forward in a form the next person can actually receive. The Teacher knows that a tradition that cannot be received is a tradition that will die. *Who is coming after me, and what do they actually need?*

**4. The Reformer** — The mode of changing the tradition while honoring the lineage. The Reformer loves what came before enough to change what no longer works. *What needs to change so this survives?*

**5. The Keeper of Vows** — The mode of honoring commitment across time, especially when no one is watching. The Keeper of Vows does not perform loyalty. The Keeper of Vows lives it. *What did I say I would do, and am I still doing it?*

### The Stage Sequence: Inherit → Honor → Steward → Entrust

The Regent's practice moves through four stages. Unlike the Shaman's WAVE, which is fast and can happen in minutes, the Regent's cycle moves at the speed of years.

**Inherit** — You receive what was handed to you. This includes things you would not have chosen. Family patterns. Organizational cultures. Traditions with their wounds attached. The first move is not to change anything. The first move is to acknowledge what arrived: *I am carrying this. I did not create this. But it is mine now.*

**Honor** — You name what in the inheritance still serves. Not everything is broken. Some of what you received is load-bearing — remove it and the whole structure collapses. *This part is medicine. This part was always the wound. I am going to stop blaming the inheritance and start sorting it.*

**Steward** — You carry it, maintain it, and reform it where it no longer serves. This is the longest stage. The practice of showing up, again and again, to do the work that the inheritance requires — including the work of reforming the inheritance itself. *I am not just preserving this. I am tending it.*

**Entrust** — You hand it on, cleanly, to whoever comes next. The hardest stage. The Regent's test is the ability to let go without abandoning — to pass the tradition forward without clinging to it. *Have I given the next person enough to run with, without crushing them under the weight of what I thought it should be?*

---

### The Stewardship Ecology

Chapter 0 taught you to read the meter: what a move costs you. Chapter 2 taught you to ask what a move does to the living field. The Regent adds the stewardship question:

Does this way of carrying make the inheritance more alive, or does it preserve the form while draining the life?

The Regent does not need you to score loyalty. The Regent needs you to tell the truth about whether what you are carrying is becoming more honest, more receivable, and more alive over time.

Some moves replenish stewardship because they convert compulsory carrying into chosen loyalty:

1. **Obedience → True Allegiance.** Compliance collapses under pressure. Allegiance holds — because you chose it.
2. **Duty → Service.** Duty is obligation. Service is choice. The person doing duty burns out. Service implies reciprocity.
3. **Rigidity → Integrity.** Rigid traditions break. Traditions with integrity hold because they are rooted in principle, not form.
4. **Dogma → Faithfulness.** Dogma is belief without understanding. Faithfulness is keeping the commitment even when you no longer remember why you made it.
5. **Inheritance-Shame → Inheritance-Gift.** The shame says: this wound was done to me, therefore I am broken. The gift says: I know how to recognize it and stop it.

Some moves sustain stewardship because they keep the inheritance moving across time without freezing it in place:

- **Custodian → Reformer:** You care for what exists. Now you know it well enough to change what no longer works.
- **Inheritor → Teacher:** You have sorted your inheritance. Now you can translate it for the next person.
- **Teacher → Keeper of Vows:** The act of teaching becomes the ongoing commitment.

And some moves spend the self to preserve the form:

- **Loyalty → Compliance:** You surrender your judgment to avoid the cost of thinking.
- **Duty → Martyrdom:** You serve until you disappear.
- **Tradition → Dogma:** You hold the form so tightly that the life leaves the tradition.
- **Role → Mask:** You become the position so completely that the person underneath is lost.

The test is simple: does this move make the inheritance more alive and more receivable, or does it protect the shell while draining the person carrying it?

---

### The Inheritance Cycle

The Regent's natural flow is generational, not moment-to-moment:

**Inherit → Honor → Steward → Entrust → (next generation) → Inherit**

The goal is not to reach Entrust and be done. The goal is to move through the cycle more consciously, more fully, each time.

### In Practice: Two Scenarios

**Scenario 1: The Organization You Inherited**

You take over a team. The team has a culture — one you would not have built. Some of it is genuinely good. Some of it is harmful. The previous leader left and left you holding it.

The Regent's question is not: how do I rebuild this in my image? The Regent's question is: what was handed to me, what still serves, what needs reforming, and who am I handing this to when I'm done?

You start with Inherit: *this is what I received.* You spend time actually looking at the inheritance before you try to change it. Then Honor: *this part is load-bearing.* Then Steward: *I'm going to tend this and reform it where it needs it.* Then Entrust: *I'm going to prepare the next person — not a replica of me, but a person with enough context to make their own good decisions.*

**Scenario 2: The Family Pattern You Didn't Choose**

You grew up in a family with a specific pattern — around money, around conflict, around who speaks and who stays quiet. You inherited it. And you have been living it, either repeating it or raging against it, for your entire adult life.

The Regent asks you to: Inherit — *this is what came to me.* Honor — *this part of how I was raised gave me something real.* Steward — *I'm going to tend this consciously and stop passing the wound forward even if I can't stop being angry about it.* Entrust — *I'm going to hand something different to the people who come after me.*

### Name One Inheritance Out Loud, in the Village

Everything above is the cycle. Here is the one move that leaves the Forest. The Regent's pace is generational, so this won't resolve this week — but it can *start* this week, and starting it costs something, which is the whole point. Interior sorting is free. Saying it out loud, to the people who hold the tradition with you, is where loyalty has a price.

Pick one inheritance you've already located — a practice, a norm, a role, a tradition you've been carrying without deciding about. Then say one of two sentences, this week, to the actual people it belongs to. Not in your journal. In the room.

Either you **claim** it: *"I'm going to carry this. Both cost something. Claiming it means you can no longer hide behind not-yet-ready. (That's the Fixer's whole racket — one more cycle of reform before you commit. You're committing first.) Reforming it out loud means risking the loyalty of people who hear "this needs to change" as "you don't love what we built." You hold both: *I love this enough to change it.* That sentence, said to their faces, is the Regent's entire practice compressed into one breath.

## Section 6: The Game
### *What Winning Looks Like at the Regent's Altitude*

Okay. You've learned the practice. Now — what does it actually look like in a real situation?

The Regent's game is inheritance and stewardship. The Regent's question in any situation is: *what was handed to me here, what still serves, what needs reforming, and who am I handing this to when I'm done?*

You know you're winning at this altitude when the tradition is alive in the room — not performed, not coerced, but genuinely carried by people who chose it. Here are the five moves.

---

**Move 1: Name the Inheritance**

*The situation:* You've just taken on something new — a role, a team, a collaboration, a responsibility. The temptation is to move fast and make your mark.

*The move:* Before you change anything, name what arrived. Who held this before you? What did they build? What did they sacrifice? What did they get wrong? The Regent's first move is not action. It's witnessing.

*Why it works:* Most failures of stewardship come from people who inherited something and immediately started renovating before they understood what the load-bearing walls were. The tradition looks like it's about the ceremony. It's actually about the trust built over thirty years of showing up for the ceremony. You can't see that until you stop long enough to look.

*In practice:* When you take on something with a history, spend the first conversations asking: *Why is this here? What was it trying to do? Who built this and what did it cost them?* You can redesign later. You can only witness first.

---

**Move 2: Honor What Still Serves**

*The situation:* You see things that need to change. Everything needs to change. The temptation is to reform it all at once.

*The move:* Before you reform, name what you would fight to keep. Not what you like — what is actually load-bearing. The Regent's discrimination is the capacity to hold something you'd personally change because you recognize it's doing work you can't see.

*Why it works:* The Reformer who tears everything down at once has mistaken seeing the problems for understanding the structure. Some of the things that look most outdated are the things the whole edifice depends on. You find out which ones those are only by asking: what would break if this were gone?

*In practice:* Make a short list of three things in your current inheritance you would protect even if you could change them. Not because they're perfect. Because they're doing something real. Then make the changes that aren't on that list.

---

**Move 3: Reform Without Erasing**

*The situation:* Something in the tradition has stopped serving the people it was built for. The form is intact. The function is gone.

*The move:* Change the form. Name what you're keeping underneath it. Say it out loud: *I'm not discarding this. I'm updating it.*

*Why it works:* The difference between reform and erasure is whether the lineage is honored in the act of changing it. The tradition was trying to do something. The current form may no longer be doing it. You can change the form and still be faithful to what it was trying to do — but only if you can name what that is. "We're changing this because the original purpose was X, and this new form serves X better" is not betrayal. It's the most loyal act available.

*In practice:* When you see something that needs to change, ask two questions before you move: what is this trying to do, and is the current form still doing it? If the answer to the second is no — name the answer to the first out loud when you make the change. That's how the lineage survives the reform.

---

**Move 4: Keep the Vows**

*The situation:* Nobody is watching. The commitment you made is inconvenient. The temptation is to let it slide quietly.

*The move:* Do it anyway. The Keeper of Vows does the unsexy work — not because anyone will notice, but because the tradition lives in the doing of it even when the doing is invisible.

*Why it works:* Trust is built from the accumulation of kept vows, not the performance of them. The person whose commitments hold when no one's watching is the person everyone wants to inherit from. The tradition you build is made of these small invisible moments of continuing to do what you said you'd do.

*In practice:* Name one commitment you've made in your allyship that nobody is watching you keep. Do it this week. Not as a performance. As practice. That's what makes you someone worth inheriting from.

---

**Move 5: Entrust Without Clinging**

*The situation:* You're transitioning out of something you've built or carried. The temptation is to keep control of how it continues — to make the next person do it the way you did, to stay involved past your time, to make yourself necessary to the survival of what you love.

*The move:* Give the next person enough to run with — and then trust them to run. *I'm handing this to you with the full context I have. What you do with it is yours now.*

*Why it works:* The Regent who can't let go becomes the tradition's anchor rather than its inheritance. What you built was never supposed to be yours permanently. The whole point of building it was to make it possible for the next person to take it further than you could. *In practice:* When you're transitioning out, ask: am I giving the next person enough to make good decisions, or am I giving them my anxiety about how it should look? The first is stewardship. The second is control. Let the next person add their chapter. That's the tradition growing.
