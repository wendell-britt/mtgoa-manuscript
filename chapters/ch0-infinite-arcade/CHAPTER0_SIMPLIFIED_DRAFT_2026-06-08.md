# Chapter 0 — The Infinite Arcade
## *The game is already running. Let's learn the controls.*

---

## Section 1: Promise

This book is three years late.

I made a promise to readers who trusted me with their money and their hope, and then I couldn't deliver. The reason, when I finally let myself see it clearly, was both embarrassing and fitting: I was writing a book about allyship, and it was making me a worse ally — not to the people I was trying to serve, but to myself, to the people closest to me, to the work itself.

I knew that helping people from a place of scarcity doesn't work. I'd built a whole framework around that idea. So I told myself I wasn't doing that. I was being responsible. I was staying in integrity. Which was all true, and also meant the guilt had nowhere to go. It went underground. The work went with it.

It wasn't until I gave myself permission to actually be mad — at myself, about the delay, about the gap between who I said I was and what I was doing — that I saw what was happening. I was holding myself hostage. Because I couldn't be perfect in the way I'd decided I had to be, I ended up doing the exact opposite of what I set out to do: making the work smaller, slower, less alive.

Seeing it clearly is what finally unlocked my ability to finish. Not fixing it. Just seeing it and deciding to keep playing anyway.

You are the person I made that promise to. This is me showing up — not perfect, not on time, but in the game.

The one thing this chapter offers is simple: a way to see the game you are already in so you can stop burning invisible fuel running the wrong one.

---

## Section 2: Diagnosis

Most people do not arrive at allyship empty-handed. They arrive carrying a question they are afraid to say out loud.

*Am I good enough? Do I matter? Am I worthy of belonging here?*

Allyship looks like an honorable place to take those questions. That is the trap. If I can be good enough at caring, maybe I can finally prove I am good. If I can join a cause important enough, maybe I can finally matter. If I can learn the right words, maybe I can delay the moment when I have to risk being seen.

That is how allyship turns into a private trial — and the other person stops being a person and becomes evidence in a case you are trying to win against yourself.

Christina Maslach's research on burnout identifies three dimensions: exhaustion, depersonalization, and inefficacy — the creeping sense that nothing you do makes a difference. What Paul Gorski's research on activist burnout adds is the specific mechanism: when the work is fused with identity, there is no mechanism for saying "I've spent enough today." The selflessness the movement culture rewards is the same mechanism that empties you out.

Here is what I have seen consistently in the people I work with: you are not burned out from too little effort. You are burned out from trying to retire a debt that was never meant to be paid off.

The debt frame is the problem. It tells you that you owe something you inherited rather than chose — and unlike a financial debt, there is no final payment. No receipt. The debt was always designed to be unpayable. Recognizing that is not giving up. It is accurate accounting.

Before we go further, bring one thing with you.

Not the abstract reason you care about allyship. The actual situation that made this book feel necessary. A relationship you do not know how to repair. A room you keep trying to hold. A person you want to help but keep exhausting yourself around. A pattern in your own care that you are tired of repeating.

Choose one. You do not have to solve it here. Just keep it nearby.

## Section 3: The Tool — Game Frame (Tokens / Tickets)

The game frame is the specific medicine for the debt — not as metaphor, but as mechanism.

A game creates healthy distance between the player and the stakes. You are playing this game. You are not the game. That distance is not detachment. It is the space that makes sustained engagement possible. The player who is fused with the outcome cannot play well. The player who has gone numb needs a game to come back into contact with what matters. One framework cannot do both. The game frame does.

James Carse drew the distinction this way: a finite game is played to win — fixed rules, defined players, a clear end. An infinite game is played to continue — the rules change to keep the game alive, the point is to keep as many people in play as possible. Allyship, as currently designed, is a finite game. There are rules. There are ways to lose. And the architecture of a finite game guarantees that eventually you hit the wall.

In an infinite game, hitting the wall is just the next move.

**Tokens — what you're spending**

Every game has a currency. In the Infinite Arcade, that currency is tokens.

**Non-renewable tokens** come from scarcity: guilt, obligation, the need to be seen as good, the debt you inherited and keep trying to pay down. They spend fast. They do not refill. You can run the account to zero and wonder why you stopped showing up — not by choice, but because the well is dry.

**Renewable tokens** come from genuine care, sustainable joy, earned capacity, the actual experience of connection being its own reward. They cost something. They also generate return. The more consciously you spend them, the more you have.

Most allyship advice assumes an infinite supply of non-renewables. Show up. Amplify. Center the marginalized. Listen more. The advice is not wrong — it is silent about the fact that every one of those actions costs something. And if you spend without replenishing, you go empty.

This is why people burn out. Not because they care too much — because they spend non-renewable tokens on games that were never designed to pay them back.

**Tickets — what you're earning**

Every game needs a reward structure. In the Infinite Arcade, your reward is the ticket.

Not applause. Not credit. Not the feeling that you did the right thing. Those come from outside the game and can be withdrawn at any time. The ticket is internal: it is the return on investment from playing well.

Here is the difference between a performance of allyship and a practice of it. A performance: you go to the meeting, say the right things, post the right thread, feel vaguely good for an hour and then empty by dinner. High cost, low return, no residue.

A practice: you go to the meeting, name what needs naming, draw the line that needs drawing. You walk out tired but clear. You sleep well. You show up for the next one. The practice replenishes as it costs — not because it is easy, but because it is yours.

The ticket system makes an honest claim: the better you play, the more you have to play with. When you draw a line from a place of clarity, the act of drawing it generates more clarity. When you repair a rupture instead of avoiding it, the repair generates more trust than you spent.

Now use what you brought. After you engage it — do you have more life available or less? That is the payout. Not what you looked like from the outside. What the move returned to the player.

**Campaigns — what the tickets fund**

Tickets are not the end of the line. You spend them. You run *campaigns* — sustained efforts in the handful of domains where your allyship actually lives. This book names those domains later; for now it is enough to know that the tickets you earn by playing well are what fund the campaigns you care about. Run on non-renewable fuel and the campaign stalls the moment you do. Run on renewable fuel and the campaign keeps moving even on the days you cannot.

**The prize — what all of it is for**

The prize is not applause. Not the finished campaign. Not being seen as good. The prize is getting to keep showing up skillfully — the capacity to play well again tomorrow without going empty. That is the whole game: spend renewable fuel, earn tickets, fund the campaigns that matter, and in doing so buy yourself more of the one thing worth having.

That is the spine the rest of the book hangs on: **tokens → tickets → campaigns → the prize.**

**The formula — a preview**

This whole book moves you through four steps:

1. identify your superpower
2. identify who needs it
3. enroll allies to help
4. show up consistently

Chapter 0 only previews the formula. The rest of the book teaches it — one Face at a time, one tool at a time. The Shaman teaches you where your superpower comes from. The Challenger teaches you where to aim it. The Regent, Architect, and Diplomat teach you how to sustain it. The Sage and Player show you how to keep the whole thing honest over time.

For now, the formula is a compass. You are here, at the beginning. The work ahead is specific and learnable.

---

**The quick version**

You do not need the whole vocabulary to start. Before any allyship move, ask one question: *what is this running on, and will it pay me back?* Renewable fuel — genuine care, earned capacity, the connection that is its own reward — spend it. Non-renewable fuel — guilt, obligation, the inherited debt — will run you to empty no matter how much you care. That one question is the frame.

## Section 4: Examples

Here is what the token distinction looks like in the rooms you already know.

You walk into a meeting where a colleague says something that erases your community's experience. The cost lands before your mind catches up. That is a token being spent. The toll is real. The cost is real. You spend another token choosing how to respond. You spend more processing it on the drive home. The meeting was ten minutes. The spending continues for hours.

Non-renewable fuel. You knew the math. You have done it in a parking garage, on a run, in the shower.

Now imagine doing that three times a day, five days a week, for years — running on guilt and obligation, without ever naming that the fuel does not renew.

Or this: you have been carrying a colleague through a hard season. You show up every week. You listen. You help. The help feels clean at first — because it is. Then something shifts. They stop asking if you are okay. The help starts to feel like a job you did not apply for. You keep showing up because stopping feels like abandonment, and abandonment would mean you are not who you think you are.

That is non-renewable fuel running a game that was never designed to pay you back. The game did not become a problem. The fuel did.

Or the inverse: you mentor someone for six months, nothing dramatic. You ask good questions. You let them make their own calls. One day they tell you that a conversation from four months ago changed how they work. You feel something uncomplicated — satisfied, not proud, not validated. Just clear. That is a ticket. That is what renewable spending returns.

The distinction is not about effort. Hard things can run on renewable fuel. Easy things can run on non-renewable fuel. The question is: what is this actually costing, and is it returning anything to the player?

## Section 5: Practice

Before you enter the next chapter, do one thing.

Use the situation you brought.

Answer three questions. Two minutes. No polish required.

**What has this cost me?** Name the tokens — not your feelings in general, but the specific ones that have been spent. Guilt. Obligation. Time you could not recover. Love that went unacknowledged. Clarity you suppressed to keep the peace.

**What has it returned?** Not what you wish it had returned. What it actually gave back to you as a player. More capacity, more clarity, more connection — or less?

**Am I in the right game?** Not is the cause right or the person worth it. Is this the game that you chose, with fuel you actually have, toward an outcome that remains possible?

You do not need a clean answer. You need to have asked the question honestly.

That is the first move. Capture it — app, paper, margin, notes app. Two minutes. Done is the point.

If you skip this, the book will keep letting you read. But the game knows the difference between reading the rules and making a move.

Make the move.

## When the Game Frame Goes Wrong

One objection deserves a straight answer: isn't calling allyship a *game* a way of trivializing real harm?

No — and here is the test. The game frame does one job: it creates enough distance between you and the stakes that you can keep playing. That distance is not detachment, and it is not permission to treat anyone's life as a move on a board. If the frame ever starts to feel like *this doesn't really matter,* you have mistaken distance for dismissal — put it down. The frame is for the player so fused with the outcome they can no longer act, and for the player so numb they need a way back into contact. It is not cover for staying comfortable while other people pay.

## Section 6: Summary and Handoff

The game is already running. It was running before you picked up this book.

The question was never whether you are in it. It was whether you knew which game you were in, and whether the fuel you were spending matched the game you were actually trying to play.

Here is what this chapter gives you:

- A way to name what you are spending (tokens)
- A way to recognize what you are earning back (tickets)
- The shape of what those tickets fund (campaigns) and what the whole thing is finally for (the prize: getting to keep showing up skillfully)
- A way to see when the game you are in was never designed to pay you back
- The beginning of the formula that the rest of this book will teach

What this chapter does not give you is permission to stop playing.

The Infinite Arcade does not promise you will win. It promises you will know which game you are in — and that knowing is the first move in playing well.

Now that you can see the game, the next question is a simpler one.

What is allyship, actually — not as an obligation, but as a practice you can sustain?

That is Chapter 1.
