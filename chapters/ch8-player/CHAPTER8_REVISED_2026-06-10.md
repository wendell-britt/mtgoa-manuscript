# Chapter 8: Creating Your Own Allyship Game
## *The Descent — From Playing the Game to Designing Your Own*

---

The Player was never exiled.

The Player left on purpose. Not because the village was wrong, not because they were rejected — because they had learned everything the village could teach, and there was something specific they wanted to build that required going further out than the village extended.

The Player had been through all six Faces and run the WAVE all the way around. Shaman, Challenger, Regent, Architect, Diplomat, Sage — each one walked, each one no longer a costume but a capacity. And when the village asked *what now?*, the Player didn't offer a reaction, or a protest, or a critique of what the village was doing wrong. The Player said: *I'm going to build something.*

The village was glad to see it. It needed someone to go first — to show that the Faces were never a destination but a toolkit, that after you learn to play all six you can design your own game.

The Player left carrying the whole map, with a specific destination in mind.

You're closer to that than you think. You've walked the Faces. You've worked the gates. You've run the WAVE in rooms that used to flatten you. The only thing left is the thing this chapter is for: to stop playing the game you were handed and start designing the one that's yours.

But something follows you out the door, and we should name it before you go, because it's the thing most likely to stop you.

---

The village taught that the six Faces were a destination — that allyship was about *becoming* a perfect Shaman, or Challenger, or Diplomat. A finished identity you arrive at and maintain. It forgot that the Faces are a map, not a menu — that you don't become one of them, you move through all of them, depending on what the moment requires.

And in that forgetting, a particular kind of Player emerges. Call it the Player in distortion.

The Player in distortion collects Faces the way people collect credentials. *I've studied with the Shaman. I've apprenticed with the Challenger. I've read the Architect's work.* They know the vocabulary. They can name the Faces, diagnose which game is being played in any room. But when it comes time to play, they're always preparing. Always about to. Always one more book away from ready.

The fatal move is collecting tools without ever building anything. Knowing all six Faces without ever choosing which one to be, right now, in this moment, at the risk of being wrong.

This is the shadow that walks out with you: *I learned all of this, and I still don't know what game to play.* The Player in distortion mistakes preparation for practice. Access for mastery. The map for the territory. And here's the trap inside the trap — it doesn't feel like avoidance. It feels like diligence. It feels like being responsible. Like you're almost ready.

If you have spent years acquiring frameworks — if some part of you came to this book hoping it would be the one that finally made you qualified — then you already know this shape from the inside. The acquiring was never the problem. The acquiring was the hiding. One more model, one more vocabulary, one more certification, and the day you actually have to do the thing keeps not arriving, because you keep it from arriving. Safely almost-ready.

And it works, as a strategy, for a long time. It keeps you safe from the one thing the acquiring was always there to protect you from — the moment you build something, put it in front of a real person, and find out whether it's any good. As long as you're still learning, that moment never comes. You get to be someone who cares deeply and is *working on it,* indefinitely. That's a comfortable place to live and an expensive one, because the cost isn't paid by you. It's paid by the people who needed the thing you were too busy preparing to build.

The real Player walks through all six Faces and then *decides.* *This is my game. This is the specific problem I'm going to solve, with the specific gifts I have, in service of the specific village I want to leave behind.* Not Faces as credentials. Faces as a design system. The difference is not knowledge. It's the willingness to build before you feel ready — which is the only way anyone has ever built anything.

And notice what moves in you when you read that, because it's the whole turn of this chapter in miniature. The almost-ready feeling isn't safety. It's a low, constant shame you've gotten so used to you stopped calling it shame — the quiet sense of being someone who knows better than they do. The moment you decide to build anyway, that shame has nothing left to feed on. It doesn't resolve by getting more ready. It resolves by moving. So name it now, the thing you've been almost-ready to build — one sentence, the project or conversation or practice you've been credentialing yourself toward instead of starting. That sentence is the first thing you'll carry down.

---

### What you're holding

So let's be precise about what you're actually holding, because it's more than you think.

A practice is not a personality type. It's not a morning routine. It's not a values statement you write once and forget.

A practice is what you actually do when something hard happens.

That's the only definition that matters. Not what you believe about allyship. Not what you intend when you're calm. What you do when you're activated — when your mother calls, when the group chat erupts, when someone says the thing that lands wrong and you have to decide, in real time, whether to speak or stay quiet. The practice is what you do when it costs you something to do it.

And you have the pieces for it now.

You know how the Shaman feels what's true — names a feeling without destroying it, lets the anger complete before acting, finds the gold in the fear before reaching for the plan. You know how the Challenger draws the line — holds a position without cruelty, is willing to be unwelcome. You know how the Regent builds what lasts — asks what's worth protecting and why, honors what came before without being imprisoned by it. You know how the Architect designs for the next person — makes the right thing easy, builds the system that hands off. You know how the Diplomat holds the field across difference — holds the tension without collapsing it, names the walk-away price before it's needed. You know how the Sage sees the whole board — knows which game is on, switches deliberately, comes back without looking down on the people still climbing.

That's not a list of things you learned. It's a set of capacities you built. And you know the WAVE that runs them — wake up, clean up, grow up, show up, come back.

You came to this book wanting to do allyship better. You may not have said it that simply — but underneath the framework-hunting was a real ache, and it probably arrived wearing one of three faces.

Maybe it was guilt — the kind that sits heavy and won't be reasoned with, that says you should be doing more, should have done more, should have known better by now. The kind that makes you tired before you start, that turns every good thing you do into a down payment on a debt that never shrinks.

Maybe it was urgency — the kind that says the world is on fire and you are sitting here reading a book about *inner work* while real people are actually suffering. The kind that makes everything feel like triage, that treats your own steadiness as a luxury you haven't earned the right to want.

Or maybe it was exhaustion — the particular kind that comes from performing allyship for a long time. Saying the right things. Posting the right takes. Being careful about every word. And finding, slowly, that none of it works — that you are doing everything right and feeling nothing right, that the care has become a job you can't quit and can't seem to do well enough.

If you came in one of those three states, you weren't failing. You were running into the wall this whole book was written to get you over: you cannot give from a place you have not gone yourself.

You may have come believing allyship was about what you did. What you said. What you posted. Who you called out. That the gap between the world and what it should be was a matter of insufficient effort, insufficient correctness.

You know now it was inward. You can't ally from a place you haven't explored. The work was in the Forest — finding what was abandoned, returning with something the village actually needs.

I came to it the way most people do. I saw something wrong, felt the discomfort of witnessing harm I couldn't fix, and did what the culture taught me — I looked outward. Learned the vocabulary. Updated my positions. Performed the correct responses. And the discomfort got worse, because I was treating the symptom with the same thinking that produced it. The shift came when I stopped asking *what should I do* and started asking *what is actually happening in me when I see this.* That's the Shaman's move. It's what this whole book has been teaching you — not what to do, but how to be capable before you do.

One honest thing before we walk. The map is honest about the terrain. It is not honest about the weather. It shows you where the gates are; it doesn't show how long it takes to walk through them, or what it feels like the third time you arrive at the same gate and find it still on duty, or the morning you realize you've been walking for months and can't remember why you started. It doesn't show you failure — the kind where you present the thing you made with care and the village says *we don't want this.* And it doesn't show success from the inside: the moment someone uses the thing you built and something opens in them that you didn't know was closed, and you feel almost nothing, because you're already thinking about the next version. The map doesn't show that the walk takes as long as it takes, that you won't finish it, that finishing was never the point. I want you to know that, so when the walking takes longer than you thought, you don't read it as proof you took the wrong path.

The village needs you for this — the actual you, not the performed one. The performed ally is easy to spot: the vocabulary, the correct positions, the performance of care without the somatic reality of having walked through the thing that hurt. The performed ally burns out, because performing care you don't yet have produces the specific exhaustion of fraud. The actual ally is harder to spot. She might not have all the right words. She might say the wrong thing and have to correct it. But she's done the work. She knows the difference between feeling and acting. She knows how to come back. The village needs her more — and you are closer to her now than you have ever been.

Feel the difference for a second, because your body already knows it. The performed ally is the version of you that leaves a hard conversation and immediately replays it, scanning for what she got wrong. The actual ally leaves the same conversation tired but clean — she said a true thing, it cost something, and there's no residue of fraud to scrub off afterward. That cleanness is the somatic marker. It's how you'll know, from the inside, which one you're being — long before you can explain it.

So. Let's walk.

### The five modes

How the building actually goes has a shape. Not a personality — a set of roles you move through, looping, each pass deeper. Five of them: Cartographer, Designer, Founder, Elder, Outlaw. Each is a different answer to the same question — *what am I actually building?*

**The Cartographer** maps your terrain before you design anything. Which Faces do you lean on? Which do you avoid? Which do you *perform* rather than embody? No judgment — just the territory. Take inventory: which one feels like home, which one have you been avoiding, which one do you perform rather than embody.

Here's my own cartography. I started by assuming I was a Diplomat. It felt like home — relational, careful, good at holding the field. But when I mapped where I actually went when things got hard, I wasn't a Diplomat at all. I was a Challenger, drawing lines. I'd been performing the Diplomat to avoid the discomfort of the Challenger's game — the discomfort of saying no, of being unwelcome, of holding a line that cost me something. The face I thought was home was a performance. The face I'd been avoiding was where I actually lived. Your map might show you something similar: the face you think you are might be the one you perform, and the face you've avoided might be the one that, built into a practice, becomes your gift.

You might not even need the full map to find the one that matters. Try this. Think of the last time you were in a genuinely hard moment and reached, automatically, for a way of being that kept you safe and kept you a little bit absent. The reach is the tell. The Face you grab under pressure is often the performance; the one you flinched away from is often the home you haven't moved into yet.

**The Designer** takes the terrain and writes the practice down — not as an abstraction, as a concrete plan. *When I'm in my Challenger, this is how I know it. When I need to move to my Shaman, this is what I do. When I'm avoiding my Regent, this is the signal.* The writing is the design; it forces you to be specific. The Designer doesn't build a perfect system — the Designer builds a working one. A practice you'll actually use beats a perfect practice you keep planning to start. The design question is always the same: *what problem am I solving, and which Face does it actually require?* Most people who avoid a Face avoid it because its territory is uncomfortable, not because it's irrelevant. The Designer asks: what am I avoiding, and what would happen if I built a practice that included it?

Here's what that looks like, stripped of abstraction. Say you map yourself as a natural Architect — you design good systems, you make clean plans, you build the structure that helps everyone else — and your gap is the Shaman, because designing is also how you get to skip feeling what's actually happening in the room. Your practice, written down, might be a single line: *Before I reach for the framework, I name the feeling out loud — mine first.* That's the whole design. Not a philosophy. A specific move, bolted to a specific trigger, that walks you into the exact Face you've been designing your way around. The design isn't the system. The design is the one sentence that puts you where you don't want to go, on purpose, before the moment makes you.

**The Founder** starts the thing that didn't exist before. Not a nonprofit, not a brand, not a platform — the specific practice this book only points toward. Name it, even if the name stays private. *This is the thing I'm building. This is the game I'm playing.* The Founder isn't the Architect; the Architect designs systems for others to use, the Founder uses all six Faces to build one practice that's theirs. Bars-engine was my Founder move — the thing I needed that didn't exist, a game that actually taught the WAVE, that made the emotional alchemy something you could practice instead of just understand. I didn't design it to be a product. I built it because I could see the gap and couldn't stop seeing it. When you're in Founder and you see something that needs to exist and nobody's building it, the only way out is to build it yourself.

For you it almost certainly won't be a game engine. It'll be smaller, more specific, more yours. The standing meeting you start because nobody is holding the space where the real conversation could happen. The document you write because the knowledge keeps dying with whoever leaves. The way you start running the team's hard conversations that becomes, without anyone deciding it, just how the team does hard conversations now. You'll know it's a Founder move and not merely a good idea the same way I did — you can't stop seeing the gap. It follows you into the shower and the commute and the middle of the night. You build the thing to make the seeing stop.

**The Elder** doesn't start new things. The Elder hands things forward. Take what you built and make it pass-onable — write it down, name the mistakes, show the next person where the traps are. The Elder's move is inheritance: not building something new, but making sure the thing you built doesn't depend on you to survive. The Elder isn't about being the authority. It's about making the work outlive you.

The test of an Elder move is brutal and simple: take yourself out of the room and see if the thing still runs. Most of what we build is secretly built to need us — it's how we stay necessary, how we keep the thing from leaving. The Elder does the opposite on purpose. You write down what only lives in your head. You name the mistake you keep almost making, out loud, so the next person doesn't have to almost make it too. You hand someone the keys before you're sure they're ready, because the only way anyone gets ready is by holding the keys. It costs something to make yourself that replaceable — it asks you to give up being the one the thing depends on. That cost is the move. An Elder who can't be replaced hasn't finished the work.

**The Outlaw** breaks the tradition the Elder just built. Finds what's calcified, names what's gone rote, challenges the structure to see if it still serves — not for its own sake, but because every tradition eventually becomes a cage, and someone has to draw the line that lets the next person breathe. This is the mode most people skip; it's uncomfortable to be the one who says *this isn't working anymore.* But the Outlaw is what keeps the Player from sliding back into the distortion — from collecting Faces as credentials, from performing the practice without doing it. You can't hand things forward without eventually having to break some of what you inherited.

And the hardest tradition to break is usually your own. The practice you designed two years ago that quietly became a cage. The role you took that everyone now expects you to keep playing. The version of allyship you got good at, the one the room rewards you for — that's the one the Outlaw has to look dead in the eye and ask whether it still serves anyone, or whether you're running it because stopping would cost you the identity you built on top of it. Breaking your own calcified game is the most uncomfortable move in this book. It's also the only thing that keeps you a Player instead of slowly becoming the next thing someone else has to learn to work around.

The modes loop. You might spend weeks in Cartographer, circle back to Designer after six months in Founder. Each pass goes deeper. The loop is the practice. The walk is the thing.

And notice which of those five just made you sit up, and which one made you want to look away. Somewhere in reading them, one felt like relief — *oh, that one's me* — and one felt like exposure, like being seen in a place you'd rather not be. That's not noise. That's your map starting to draw itself. The relief is the mode you can build from today. The exposure is the edge you've been routing around, which means it's exactly where your next real move is. Name them both before you read on — the one that felt like home, the one that felt like exposure. Two words. That's the first honest line of your design, and it cost you nothing but the willingness to notice.

### The walk

Now the walk itself — what it actually feels like to build with the map in your hand.

The walk is not upward. The walk is forward. I want to be honest about what it looks like, because the book would be doing you a disservice if it ended with a rousing speech about your potential and left you to figure out the rest alone.

The walk begins before you're ready. That's the first thing nobody tells you. You don't finish learning the Faces and then start building — you start building while you're still confused, still triggered, still defaulting to your home game when things get hard. You act from somewhere on the path, and the acting is part of the walking.

When I started building bars-engine, I didn't have it figured out. I had a felt sense of what was missing and enough of the six Faces to know what I was trying to do. But the thing itself wasn't clear. The mechanics kept changing. I ran sessions that failed. I rebuilt the token economy three times. The walk was not a straight line from plan to execution — it was iteration, and each circle went a little deeper. The iteration wasn't a detour from the walk. The iteration *was* the walk. The first design was the walk. The third redesign was the walk. The version that failed in front of people was the walk.

And here's what the walk is not: it is not the elimination of fear. You'll still be afraid — when you build something and it doesn't work, when the village doesn't receive it the way you hoped, when someone critiques the thing you made and they're right and you have to decide whether to defend it or redesign it.

Here's what I've noticed about my own fear of building: it rehearses. The fear runs the entire scenario — the failure, the criticism, the public collapse — before I've taken a single real action. The fear isn't a signal that says *don't do this.* It's a signal that says *this matters enough to be afraid of.* The question is never *should I be afraid.* It's *what am I going to do with the fear* — and the answer is to use the rehearsal as data. The fear is showing me what I care about, which means it's showing me what I'm trying to build.

But the rehearsal is not the thing. The mind running failure is not the same as doing the thing. It produces the same anxiety whether you've done nothing or whether the thing has already failed. The rehearsal feels like work. It is not work. The actual thing is simpler and scarier: put it in front of someone and see what happens.

This is the WAVE in your life now, not a meditation you do in the morning. Wake up to what's happening in you when the news lands. Clean up — run the channel, let the feeling complete. Grow up — ask what this moment is asking you to become. Show up — act from that place, not the reactive one the old habit would have sent you to. Come back — notice what shifted. It runs through your parenting, your partnership, your work, the group chat, the election, whatever is next. It runs through the writing of this book: I wake up to what's actually happening in a chapter, clean up the places I'm performing instead of saying what's true, grow up into the harder version of the idea I was avoiding, show up to the page, and come back to find the chapter that blocked me for three days unclogged the moment I let the Shaman name what was there.

The walk also means walking into the specific problem you're trying to solve. You didn't come to this book with a general question. You came with a specific ache — some place in your life or work or family where the allyship you were performing wasn't working and you knew it. Maybe a relationship where you kept doing the right thing and feeling nothing. Maybe a community where you were the designated ally and it was destroying you. Maybe a public issue where you felt morally obligated to act and emotionally unable to and confused about what action even meant anymore.

The walk is walking into *that* problem. Not a cleaner version of it. The actual one. My specific problem is the one bars-engine is built to solve: people who've done the therapy and read the books and know the vocabulary, and who still don't have a practice — who still default to their home game when it's hard, who still mistake understanding for transformation. The gap between knowing and being able to do. Everything I build is me walking into that gap.

Yours has a shape too, even if you've never said it in a single sentence. It's the thing you keep circling. The conversation you keep almost having. The pattern in your family or your team or your community that you can see clearly and still haven't found the move for. It's probably something that costs you a little to look at directly — which is exactly how you know it's yours and not a borrowed cause you picked up because it was the one you were supposed to care about. Don't reach for the version that sounds important. Reach for the one that's true: the specific ache in the specific place, the one with a face attached to it.

What's yours?

Some of you are resisting one of those words — *Founder.* *I don't start things. I'm not an entrepreneur. I'm a helper, a parent, a friend.* I hear that. Founder isn't a personality type. It's a mode, and it activates the moment you decide to create something that didn't exist before you — a conversation, a family tradition, a way of showing up at work nobody had named. You've already done this. A tradition you started. A conversation you had differently than your parents would have. A way of being with your kids that broke a pattern. You've already been the Founder; you just didn't call it that. The question isn't *are you a founder type.* It's *are you willing to do it on purpose.* You don't have to build bars-engine. You don't need a platform or funding or an audience. You decide the problem you're carrying is worth solving, and you start making something toward it. The scale doesn't matter. The specificity does.

And some of you don't have the game yet — still in the confusion, still in the iteration, still asking *but what is mine?* That's not the distortion. The distortion uses *I don't know* as a place to stay. You can use it as a place to start. You might be a Cartographer who hasn't become a Founder yet — keep the inventory honest, keep noticing the gap that keeps showing up, and when you can name it in a sentence, the Founder move becomes obvious. The question isn't *do you have a complete game.* It's *are you walking.* The walk produces the game.

And the walk includes failure — not as a lesson about resilience, as an actual thing that happens. Let me tell you about one of mine, because the abstract version lets you off too easy. I built a version of bars-engine once that I was sure was right — clean mechanics, an elegant loop, the whole thing humming in my head. I ran it with real people, and it died in the room. Not dramatically. Worse than dramatically: politely. People were confused, then bored, then kind about it. The thing I'd been protecting for months turned out to solve a problem *I* had and not one *they* had. That's a specific kind of awful — not that you were rejected, but that you were right about everything except the only thing that mattered.

Here's what I did with it, eventually, after the part where I wanted to quit and the part where I blamed the players. I ran the WAVE on it. Woke up to what actually happened, instead of the story where they just didn't get it. Cleaned up the mechanics that were serving my cleverness instead of their experience. Grew up into the harder version — the one that made me give up the elegant thing I was attached to. Showed up with the next build. It was better, and it was only better because I finally treated the failure as data instead of as a verdict on me. Some failures are just failures: the design was wrong, the timing was wrong, the thing didn't solve what you thought it would. That's not defeat. That's the most honest information the work will ever hand you.

The walk ends the way all walks end — with you having gone further than you started, carrying something you didn't have before. You won't have a perfect map. You'll still default to your home game when it's hard. You'll still avoid the uncomfortable Face. That's not failure. That's the walk. What the village needs from you isn't perfection. It's the willingness to be the one who goes first — not because you have the answers, but because you have the map, and you're willing to walk it and report back.

### The first move

Before you go any further down, one design and one doing. Within seven days.

The Player's whole game is the designed move — the one you can say in a single sentence, the one that fits in a moment, the one you can make without anyone's permission. So design it. Pick the situation: the one that keeps coming back, the one you keep failing at the same way. Pick the move — one sentence. *When this shows up, I do this, instead of what I always do.* Say it out loud. Write it down. Set the alarm. Do whatever makes the move remember itself when the moment arrives.

That's the design, and the design is free. The doing is the move. Within seven days, let the situation come back — it will — and do the move. Sixty seconds. You are not asked to be good at it. You are asked to do it.

There are two ways this goes, and only one of them is the Player. One: you designed the move, the situation showed up, you did it — and the next time it comes back, the move is the path of least resistance. The groove is cut. Two: you designed the move beautifully, the situation didn't quite show up inside the window, and you felt that small relief of having designed without having been tested — and the move is still just a design. That second one is the Player's exact shadow: the elegant move kept safely offstage. The whole game is the design meeting the moment. Don't let it stay a proposal.

Here's mine, so you know the scale we're talking about. The situation I kept failing at: a partner escalating in a meeting, and me managing it, smoothing it, performing the calm. The move I designed was one sentence, said out loud: *"I think we're not arguing about the slide."* That's it. That's the whole move. I've done it four times now. The first time my hands shook. The fourth time it was just a thing I do. The meeting is the same meeting. The person is the same person. I'm the same person. The move is what changed.

And here's what nobody warns you about: the move you've been dreading is almost never as bad as the rehearsal made it. You'll do it, and it'll be awkward or smaller than you pictured, and underneath the nerves you'll catch something you forgot was there — it felt *good.* Not the relief of having it over. The aliveness of doing the thing you're for. Catch that before the doubt re-narrates it into "that was nothing." That feeling is the data. It's the Child telling you it wants to come out and play — and that it has been waiting a long time to be asked.

*The situation. The move. The first time you did it. What it cost. What it became the fourth time. → app*

---

There's one question that tells you whether any of this is real — and it has nothing to do with how productive you feel. It's about direction: is the work moving *toward* contact with a person, or *away* from it?

Genuine building points outward. You make the thing so it can meet someone — a reader, a colleague, your kid. You want it in front of them, even though that's the scary part. The work is going somewhere, and the somewhere has a face.

The distortion builds in the other direction — inward, in circles, away from anyone who might actually receive it. Endless revisions. Infinite drafts. Redesigning the same mechanic six times, and every redesign a way of not doing the one thing the work needs: putting it in front of someone and seeing what happens. It looks like progress. It's a very sophisticated way of hiding.

So keep the question, because planning-disguised-as-building can't survive it: *is this moving toward someone, or away from them?* Real building gets handed over, even unfinished. Avoidance keeps the thing safe, and perfect, and unmet.

Hold that question. You'll need it the rest of your life as a Player. But right now there's one more step, and it's the one the whole book has been walking toward.

---

Before the last step, look at what's in your hands.

When you started this book, the sheet was almost all blank. You filled the first lines in the dark — the game you were playing, the pattern you defaulted to, the one belief you chose to run the whole way down. You couldn't have filled the rest then. You hadn't earned the answers.

Look at it now.

*Your superpower.* Look at the line. You couldn't write it at the beginning — you tried, and it came out as a wish, a thing you hoped you were good at. It isn't a wish now. You traced it back to the belief that built it: the capacity you over-developed *because* some part of you was certain you weren't ready, or weren't enough, or didn't belong, and had to compensate so hard that the compensation became a gift. That's the cruel economy of it — and it's also the good news, because the thing you were most ashamed of needing turned out to be the thing the people around you most need from you. Made conscious, turned outward, that's the first move of your Formula. You can name it now. You can name what it cost. The line is in your own handwriting.

*Who needs it.* Look at the name. Not a category — categories are where the doubt hides, where *I want to help marginalized communities* lets you off the hook forever. One person. One situation. One room where your specific capacity meets a specific need, where if you brought the thing you just named, something would actually change. The Challenger made you write the name even though writing it made it real and a little frightening. It's still there on the page. That's the second move, and it's the one that turns your superpower from a quality you have into a thing you *do, for someone.*

*Who you enroll.* Look at this line, because it changed the most. You arrived doing this alone — performing for an authority you never chose, carrying what you carried because some inherited Regent decided long ago that it was yours to carry. Somewhere in the middle of this you stopped, and decided whose work this actually is, and made it real out loud in a conversation with another person. You found the one leverage point — the smallest change that makes the largest difference for someone you're allying with. That's the third move. And in a moment you're going to find out it has one more entry on it that you never wrote, that was always there, that you simply couldn't see yet.

*How you show up.* Look at the last line. Not the heroic version — the repeatable one. Your one honest term: the sentence that names what your staying requires to remain chosen instead of performed. *Here is what this requires of me. Here is what I need for it to stay real.* You wrote it about a real relationship, with real stakes, which is why it's the hardest line on the sheet and the one that will keep you in the room longest. Not the ally who burned bright and burned out. The one who's still there next month, because she built her staying on something true instead of something owed.
