# 6-Face GM Analysis — Deck Spec V1
## *Diagnosis: What Each Face Sees as Broken, Missing, and Right*

**Source:** WENDELL_ALLY_DECK_SPEC.md, 2026-04-28
**Method:** 6 Game Master Faces applied to spec document
**Purpose:** Identify what the spec still holds from earlier frames that don't work

---

## 🧠 Architect — Structure and Integrity

**What I see working:**
The three-component card (Recognition / Move / Bridge) is the right architecture. It solves the solo-play problem cleanly. The two-taxonomy separation (suits vs. domains) is architecturally sound and necessary. The three-test framework is exactly right — a card that can't pass all three should not exist.
 
**What I see breaking:**

The suit arc still has a sequencing problem. Clean Up is positioned as dissolving the ally's own self-sabotaging beliefs, but the interview said: *"If your target has self-sabotage, none of your allyship will land."* That means Clean Up is not just about the ally's beliefs — it's about recognizing when the PERSON THEY'RE HELPING has self-sabotage and adjusting the approach accordingly. The ♦️ suit currently serves only one function (self-clearing). It should serve two: self-clearing AND target-recognition. These are different moves requiring different cards.

The Grow Up suit (A-10) describes internal capacity work that doesn't map to the field-guide use case. "Set your stuff aside without suppressing it" is a therapy move, not a mid-conversation move. The deck says it should work in the conversation — but the Grow Up cards as written would require a quiet room and 20 minutes of self-inquiry. The Architect says: if the card requires more than one sentence of action, it fails Test 2. The Grow Up suit as written fails Test 2 on most cards.

**The structural fix:**
- ♦️ Clean Up: split into two modes — self-clearing (ally's beliefs) AND target-reading (recognizing when the other person's self-sabotage is in the room)
- ♥️ Grow Up: rewritten to be mid-conversation capacity moves, not journal entries
- The spec says "♣️ Wake Up first" but the spec's own card ordering suggests ♦️ Clean Up should be first — because you need to know what you're working with before you listen. Or at minimum: the cards themselves should decide their order, not the spec writer.

## 🏛 Regent — What the Spec Preserves That Should Stay

**What I see working:**
The physical form constraint ($25-35, poker size, matte finish) is correct. The phone page as "digital twin" not "separate product" is the right call — it prevents the product from becoming two half-built things. The "three tests per card" rule is load-bearing and should be treated as law.

**What I see preserving that should be examined:**

The card count (52) is being treated as a constraint that must be met, but the field guide format doesn't need 52 cards. A field guide needs the RIGHT cards, not a full deck. The "every suit gets A-10 + J/Q/K" structure is inherited from playing card convention, not from the use case. If the ♥️ Grow Up suit only has 4 useful cards, it should have 4 cards. The spec should not force a full deck.

The "solo-playable first, group play emergent" structure is correct and should stay. The "entry trigger is the moment" framing is correct and should stay. The "specific deck before general deck" sequencing is correct and should stay.

**The Regent's concern:**
The marketing section (Section 6) is too earnest. "The deck positions itself as a practical tool, not a self-improvement program" — this is defensive positioning. The deck that actually works won't need to disavow self-improvement. It will just be useful. The language about what the deck "does not assume" (therapy, book reading, formal context, capacity) is actually the marketing copy — it should be front and center, not buried in the spec's assumptions section.

## ⚔️ Challenger — What the Spec Still Gets Wrong

**What I see breaking:**

The Clean Up suit (Section 3.5, ♦️ A-10) lists the six beliefs as card titles: "I'm not good enough / I'm not ready / I don't belong / I'm insignificant / I'm not worthy / I'm not capable." This is almost right, but it names the beliefs as if the ally is working on themselves. But the interview said: *"Clean up also unblocks the allyship target. If your target has self-sabotage, none of your allyship will land."* The six beliefs need a second set of cards — not "I don't belong" (working on yourself) but "they don't belong" (recognizing when the person you're helping is being blocked by their own beliefs, not by external obstacles. This is target-reading, not self-clearing.

The Grow Up suit is still treating internal capacity as the work. But the interview's definition of Grow Up is: "increasing one's capacity and developmental level." The spec's Grow Up cards (listen to something involving your own material, stay with discomfort without fixing it) are therapy skills, not allyship skills. The Grow Up move in allyship is: being able to hear something that challenges your worldview and staying in the conversation. Not self-management — worldview expansion.

**The Challenger's direct challenge:**
The spec still has "♣️ Wake Up → Clean Up → Grow Up → Show Up" as a linear sequence. But the field guide use case is not linear. In a moment, you might need to: recognize that YOU are the one with self-sabotage blocking (♦️), stay in the conversation despite discomfort (♥️), and make a specific offer (♠️) — all in the same 3-minute window. The spec's sequential framing will produce sequential cards. Sequential cards can't serve a non-linear moment. The suit order needs to be rethought as simultaneous rather than sequential.

**What I would cut:**
The "◆◆◆ Hard = edge territory" framing implies that the difficulty variants are escalating toward something harder. Field guides don't escalate difficulty — they escalate relevance. A "hard" card is not harder to do — it's for a more complex moment. The bullet density should signal complexity of moment, not intensity of self-work. ◆ = routine moment. ◆◆ = complex moment. ◆◆◆ = crisis moment.

## 🎭 Diplomat — What Bridges and What Doesn't

**What I see working:**
The "How to Be an Ally to Wendell" product name is the Diplomat's win. It names a specific relationship and lets the general principle emerge from it. The two-taxonomy separation (suit = type of move, domain = area of impact) is a bridge between the MTGOA framework and the reader's actual experience. Readers don't think in Kotter stages — but they do think in "I need to raise awareness vs. I need to organize something."

**What I see failing to bridge:**

The "Allyship use" component (bottom of card) is the right idea with the wrong implementation. The spec says it should be "the bridge from doing the card to being a better ally." But a single sentence can't bridge anything — it's too short to connect the internal move to the relational outcome. The bridge needs two sentences: one about what you just did, one about what it made possible in the relationship. "Catching the performance before it looks like care is the start of actually being useful" — this is the internal move described, not the relational outcome.

The Diplomat would rewrite the Allyship use component as: "What just changed between you and the person you're helping." Not a principle. A observed effect.

**The Diplomat's specific concern:**
The "◆ Easy / ◆◆ Medium / ◆◆◆ Hard" notation uses bullet density. This will not survive translation to the phone page or to a printed card where bullet density might not render. The difficulty variants need a notation that works in all formats. Consider: "Routine / Complex / Crisis" — these map to actual moments and work in every format.

## 🌊 Shaman — The Felt Sense of the Spec

**What I see in the spec's shadow:**

The spec is trying to solve too many problems at once. It's trying to be: a field guide AND a therapeutic tool AND a marketing document AND a card game. The Shaman says: pick one primary function and let everything else be emergent from that. The primary function is: "have the right move in the moment." If a card can't be used in the moment, it doesn't belong in this deck — regardless of how therapeutically useful it might be.

The spec treats the EA labels as "internal design tool" — this is correct, but the spec doesn't explain HOW to use them as a design tool. Without that explanation, the EA labels will either get forgotten or get surfaced in ways that break Test 1 (Recognition). The Shaman would add one sentence to Section 5: "Use the EA labels to audit emotional range across the full deck — every reading should be able to find a card that matches the emotional temperature of the moment they're in."

**What the Shaman feels is missing:**

The felt sense of the spec is: competent, structured, slightly clinical. The deck it's describing will be competent and structured. But the deck it's describing won't have warmth. The target user (Integral Green White Female, Enneagram 2, fawning patterns) will respond to warmth before she responds to structure. The spec should say somewhere: "The language of each card should feel like a friend who knows what they're doing — not a therapist, not a manual, a friend."

## 📖 Sage — What Would Make This Deck Irreplaceable

**The pattern I see:**

The spec is solving for "how do we write good cards" — but it's not yet solving for "what would make someone reach for this deck instead of Googling or asking a friend or just guessing."

The answer to that question is: SPECIFICITY. The cards that will work are the ones that name something so precisely that the reader thinks: "I didn't have words for that before, and now I do, and that changes everything."

**What the Sage says is missing:**

The recognition prompt (Component 1) is the most important part of the card — and the spec gives the least guidance on how to write it. "Answerable in 10 seconds" is a constraint, not a method. The Sage would add a design rule:

> Recognition prompts work by naming the specific failure mode, not the general principle. Not: "Are you performing care?" But: "Did you just offer something vague and feel depleted afterward?" The recognition prompt's job is to make the moment recognizable. It should use the reader's own language, not the framework's language.

The Sage would also flag: the Grow Up suit as written is the ally expanding THEIR worldview — hearing something that challenges what they believe and staying. This is not self-management. This is perspective expansion. The cards should reflect this.

**The question the Sage would ask before any cards get written:**

*"What is the one moment — specific, real, named — that this deck exists to serve?"*

If the answer is a real interaction Wendell has had, those are the cards to write first. Not the abstract suite structure. The real moment.

## The One Fix That Would Solve the Most

**Split ♦️ Clean Up into two modes.** The interview is clear: "If your target has self-sabotage, none of your allyship will land." This means the ally needs to be able to READ the other person's self-sabotage, not just clean their own. The current spec only does the second. The first requires different cards, different recognition prompts, and a different use case.

The split:

**♦️ Clean Up (self):** "Is MY self-sabotage blocking this allyship?" → cards that surface and dissolve the 6 beliefs in the ally
**♦️ Clean Up (target):** "Does THE PERSON I'm helping have self-sabotage blocking them?" → cards that recognize the pattern in the other, adjust the approach, and find the crack where allyship can actually land

This split would make the deck genuinely different from every other allyship resource. Most allyship training asks: "do you have the right information?" This deck asks: "do you have the right recognition?" The target-reading mode is the differentiator.
