# AI + Humanism in Game Design — Diplomat + Architect Analysis
## *Who Is Getting This Right, and What Are They Actually Doing?*

**Research scope:** Game designers and studios at the intersection of AI capability and human creative direction — specifically those using AI as studio-not-artist, with taste as the art director.

---

## The People Getting This Right

### 1. Todd Howard / Bethesda
**What they're doing:** Using AI for procedural generation the same way they've used it for decades — to move set pieces around, generate dungeon layouts, scale content. The AI extends what procedural generation always did. Howard's position: *"handcrafted human intention is where the value is."*

**The Diplomat's take:** Howard is bridging two worlds — the studio that built Morrowind's handcrafted provinces and the studio that's now shipping Starfield with AI-generated planets. He doesn't see AI as a threat to craft. He sees it as a new layer of procedural tooling that still requires the human to decide what the *rules* are. This is the most mainstream, big-studio version of the taste-as-director model. It works because Bethesda's culture already had a relationship with procedural generation — AI is just the next iteration.

**The Architect's take:** This is spec-able. Bethesda's model works because they defined the *rules* of the procedural system before the AI touched it. The dungeon generation has parameters. The planet generation has constraints. Those parameters are the design spec. AI generates within those parameters. The approval gate is: does this output fall within the defined rules?

**Application to our deck:** We need to define the *rules* of our card design system before any AI touches it. The rules are not aesthetic — they're structural. The suit symbol has this geometry. The recognition prompt is this size. The move is this weight. Once the rules are defined, AI can generate within them. Human approves against the rules.

---

### 2. Hello Games / No Man's Sky
**What they're doing:** Managing planetary biome diversity and ecology with AI — ensuring that each of 18 quintillion planets feels distinct and explorable. The scale is AI-generated. The *feel* is human-designed.

**The Diplomat's take:** Hello Games is the proof that massive scale + human curation can coexist. The player feels like they're discovering something real on each planet. That's not because the AI generated interesting planets. It's because the humans defined the rules that make a planet feel worth discovering. The AI executed at scale. The human designed the taste that made the scale matter.

**The Architect's take:** The lesson for our deck: scale is not the enemy of taste. 52 cards with consistent design language is not harder than 1 card. It's the same problem — define the system, execute consistently, approve against the rules. The deck's visual consistency depends on the system being defined clearly enough that AI can generate within it without producing the "AI look."

**Application:** The system is the art. Define it precisely. The precision is what prevents AI-generic output.

---

### 3. Pim de Witte / General Intuition ($134M raised)
**What they're doing:** Rethinking how games are built from the ground up. Key insight from their framework: *"Experience and Game Design: What new fun and interactive experiences can now be crafted for the player?"*

**The Diplomat's take:** This is the most forward-looking version of the AI + humanism model. De Witte is not using AI to make existing game development faster. He's using AI to ask: what games can we now *imagine* that we couldn't before? This is the creative question — not "how do we do what we were doing with AI?" but "what becomes possible now?"

**The Architect's take:** The two-part framework is the right spec structure: (1) Search space of possibilities — what can the AI generate? (2) Experience design — what do we want the player to feel? The gap between those two is where taste operates. The spec needs to document both sides — what the system can produce, and what experience it's designed to create.

**Application to our deck:** The shuffle page is this. The AI can generate a random card draw. The experience we're designing is: receiving a card that feels like a message from someone who knows you. The AI provides the randomness. The human designed the warmth.

---

### 4. Chris Do / The Futur
**What they're saying:** *"AI can give you answers. But only you can bring taste."*

**The Diplomat's take:** This is the clearest articulation of the model I've found. AI produces options. Taste chooses. The person who survives is not the one who can generate faster — everyone can do that now. The person who survives is the one who knows what to make.

**The Architect's take:** The corollary is: if taste is the differentiator, then taste is the skill that needs to be developed, documented, and spec-ed. The design brief needs to document *why* the deck looks the way it looks — not just what the visual choices are, but what emotional logic led to them. An AI or contractor who reads the spec should be able to make consistent decisions without Wendell in the room for every approval. The spec needs to transfer taste, not just instructions.

**Application:** The brand voice doc is not a style guide. It's a taste transfer document. It needs to explain *why* — not just what.

### 5. The SCAD / Creative Bloq Research
**What it says:** "Human-curated art direction will ultimately win" and "Taste and Creative Judgment" is a learnable skill in AI-native design roles.

**The Architect's take:** This is the confirmation that our approach is correct and also that the market knows it. Design skills have surpassed coding as the most in-demand competency in AI-specific job listings. The human-centered thinking is more valued, not less. This means: investing in taste documentation is not a nice-to-have. It's the core competency.

**The Diplomat's take:** The cultural moment supports this deck. The market is saturated with "use AI to look helpful" products. The products that will survive are the ones that feel like they came from a specific person with specific taste. The deck is exactly that — "made by someone who actually knows what they're talking about."

## The Specific Mechanisms That Work

### Human-as-art-director, AI-as-studio
The consistent pattern across all these examples:
1. Human defines the rules / parameters / emotional logic
2. AI generates within those parameters
3. Human approves against the rules (not against "does it look good" — against "does this fall within what we defined as right")

The approval question is: **does this match the taste we documented?** Not: **does this look nice?**

### The taste documentation problem
The gap between studios that do this well and studios that don't is: taste documentation. Bethesda can hand their procedural rules to any AI and get consistent output because the rules are documented. Hello Games can generate 18 quintillion planets with consistent feel because the feel was defined before the generation started.

The failure mode is: trying to approve AI output against "I know it when I see it" — which requires the human to be in the room for every decision.

### Scale doesn't ruin taste
The lesson from Hello Games: 18 quintillion planets doesn't feel generic because the taste was defined at the system level, not at the individual planet level. The deck's 52 cards won't feel generic if the taste is defined at the system level — not if Wendell approves each card individually.

The system is the art director. The human is the veto.

## What This Means for Our Deck

### The design kit needs to document taste at the system level

The deck's visual language needs to be documentable as rules — not "this card looks right" but "this is the rule that makes this card right." The rules allow AI to generate and allow contractors to execute without Wendell in every room.

### The approval gate is taste-based, not aesthetic

The question at approval is not "does this look good?" It's "does this fall within the rules we defined?" The rules are documented in the design kit. The veto power is human. The system produces consistent output.

### The shuffle page is the most AI-native moment

The AI's job is to be unpredictable. That's the point. The shuffle page is where the deck is most like a game — random draw, oracle energy, the pause before revelation. The AI generates the randomness. The human designed the warmth that makes the randomness feel like a message, not a glitch.

### The competitive moat is specific taste at scale

The deck doesn't need to look like no deck ever made. It needs to look like this deck — specific, warm, precise. 52 cards with consistent taste is hard. That's the work. That's the moat.

## Key Insight: The Design Brief Is the Product

The most important thing Hello Games and Bethesda share: the procedural rules came before the generation. The design system — the taste documented as rules — is what makes the product consistent.

Our deck's competitive moat is not the card content. It's not the visual design. It's the design kit that documents why the deck looks and feels the way it does. That kit is the reusable asset — it can guide the physical deck, the digital app, the QR pages, the audio notes, the bars-engine integration.

The kit is the product. The deck is an output of the kit.
