## What We Have So Far

**Suit architecture (interview-derived):**
- ♣️ Wake Up → The listening (tracking where Wendell is AND where he's trying to go)
- ♦️ Clean Up → Dissolving 6 self-sabotaging beliefs that block good listening
- ♥️ Grow Up → Expanding capacity to listen to things involving your own material (discipline: not attached, not dissociated)
- ♠️ Show Up → Doing the work (signature response mechanic + honest handoff)

**Core mechanic:** Triage — know your capacity, know their domain, make the match.

**Failure mode the deck must break:** "How can I help?" as a blank check that puts direction burden on the person who needs help.

## 🧠 Architect — The Structural Diagnosis

**What I see:** This interview gave us a clean architectural skeleton. Four suits map cleanly to four domains. The question is whether the suit order matches the ally's learning arc or just the domain taxonomy. These are different things.

**The key question Architect is sitting with:**

> Does Wake Up → Clean Up → Grow Up → Show Up represent a learnable sequence? Or does the person who needs Clean Up most often fail at Wake Up first, making the sequence aspirational rather than developmental?

**What the Architect notices is missing from the interview findings:**

1. **Entry criteria.** How does someone know they need this deck? What's the trigger that says "I'm about to be a bad ally if I don't do this work first"? Without an entry trigger, people who need Clean Up will skip to Show Up because it's more satisfying.

2. **The 6 self-sabotaging beliefs.** Six specific blockers were named. Are all six equal? Or is there a hierarchy — where if you fix blocker 1 (threatened by what you might hear), blockers 2-5 become less potent? The deck needs to know which blocker is the load-bearing one.

3. **The skill-vs.-trait question.** "Listening is a skill. They can build it." But what's the practice vehicle? Skills are built through repetition under conditions of increasing difficulty. What's the practice loop for listening?

4. **The "fire and peace" energy constraint.** This was named as the signature of good allyship energy. But it's a feeling, not a metric. How do you know when you've got it? And if you don't have it yet, what do you practice to get there?

5. **The domains of impact question.** The four domains were named as the triage structure (awareness, resources, organizing, direct action). But do the four suit names map to these four domains, or are they separate from the Kotter change stages? If they're separate, the deck has two taxonomies running — and they'll compete for space.

**Architect's structural concern:** The interview gave us a content map (what's needed). We don't yet have a sequencing map (what comes first that makes the rest possible). That's the next design question.

## 🏛 Regent — What to Keep and Not Touch

**What I see:** Some things in this interview are clean and should not be revised out of existence. They passed the "is this true and specific?" test. The Regent's job is to name them.

**The following should be preserved exactly as stated:**

1. **The signature response mechanic.** Three steps: "I hear X / I relate to X because Y / Let's partner together toward resolution." This is teachable, replicable, and doesn't require the person to be a therapist or an expert. It's a specific format with a specific function. Do not generalize it. Do not make it optional. Put it in the deck verbatim.

2. **"Until I was done."** The helper doesn't decide when the listening is complete. The person being helped does. This is the single most important thing the deck could teach. It corrects for the most common failure mode: the helper who manages the conversation to their own comfort level.

3. **The honest handoff.** "I can't help with X but I've got spoons for Y." This is the thing that makes the handoff not a rejection. The person isn't saying no. They're saying "not this, but something." That's what makes the follow-up a team move rather than an abandonment.

4. **"Triage" as the frame.** The deck doesn't teach people to be everything. It teaches them to do one thing well. The triage frame (self-triage first, then match to their domain) is the correct frame for a deck that serves the person who wants to be an ally but doesn't know their own capacity.

5. **"Fire and peace" as the energy signature.** Not "calm" (which is just suppressed), not "hype" (which is anxious energy that hasn't been processed). Fire is genuine care that shows up as momentum. Peace is resourcing that doesn't make the other person manage them. This constraint, if embedded in the design, would distinguish this deck from every other allyship resource on the market.

**Regent's concern:** The "fire and peace" constraint is the hardest thing to hold across a full deck. As cards get written, they'll tend toward "calm helpful energy" — which is not the same thing. The deck needs a rule that says: if this card could be read as "be calm and supportive," it's wrong.

## ⚔️ Challenger — What to Reject

**What I see:** The interview reveals patterns in how allyship culture has failed. The Challenger's job is to name what the deck must explicitly reject — so that when people read it, they know what it's not.

**Reject the following explicitly:**

1. **"How can I help?" as an opening question.** The Challenger would have written this question out of the deck on sight. It's the number one failure mode. It puts the burden of direction on the person who needs help. It makes the helper feel like they did something when they asked it. It produces zero useful information. The deck should not improve this question. It should replace it.

2. **"Let me know if there's anything I can do."** This is the allyship participation trophy. It looks like an offer. It's actually a withdrawal. The person is making a social gesture without committing resources. The deck should name this directly and teach the specific alternative: come with a specific offer from your actual capacity.

3. **Allyship as identity, not practice.** "I am an ally" is a statement that closes the conversation. It says the work is done. The deck should not produce allies. It should produce people who show up in specific ways for specific purposes. The noun is the trap. The verb is the point.

4. **The idea that good allyship is a feeling.** "I want to help" is not the same as "I helped." The deck should not optimize for good intentions. It should optimize for the person on the other end being helped. The test is not "did you feel like a good ally?" It's "did Wendell get what he needed?"

5. **Generosity without capacity verification.** The rent party worked because Rhea had checked her capacity (she knew she could throw a party), had a specific offer (the party format + fundraising structure), and was offering something she genuinely enjoyed (not charity, not rescue). The deck must never produce the version of that where Rhea shows up anxious, half-committed, and looking to Wendell to figure out the rest.

**Challenger's challenge:** Every one of the rejected patterns will show up in early card drafts. The deck will drift toward "be kind, be present, be supportive" because that's what the dominant allyship culture expects. The Challenger's discipline is to cut every card that could be read as advice for being a good person rather than a specific move in a specific moment.

## 🎭 Diplomat — What This Deck Must Bridge

**What I see:** This deck lives in a specific tension: it's designed FOR a specific person (Wendell) but it's meant to be USABLE by anyone. The Diplomat's job is to name the bridging work the deck has to do — the space between "what works for Wendell" and "what can a homie from the neighborhood actually practice."

**The bridging problems:**

1. **The specific-to-general extraction problem.** The interview gave us material that works for Wendell. The goal is to extract what's generalizable. But "generalizable" is not the same as "vague." The extraction should produce patterns that hold across allyship contexts — not a diluted version of Wendell's specific needs.

2. **The capacity-matching problem.** The deck teaches people to describe their own capacity accurately. But most people who want to be good allies have never been asked to do this. They've been taught that good allyship means being available and flexible. The deck has to reverse this belief in the space of a card reading. That's a heavy lift for a single card.

3. **The emotional containment problem.** Good listening requires the ally to "not be attached to their own stuff but not be dissociated." This is a high-level skill. Most people are either dissociated (not feeling what the other person is feeling, offering solutions from a distance) or over-attached (carrying the other person's feelings as their own, becoming depleted). The space between those two is small and hard to find. The deck has to teach people to find it.

4. **The fire-and-peace energy problem.** This is the deck's aesthetic constraint. But most people don't know what "fire and peace" feels like in themselves. They know what anxious helpfulness feels like. They know what calm detachment feels like. The deck needs to give them a way to distinguish their energy signature before they can manage it.

**Diplomat's bridge question:**

> Is the deck's primary audience people who want to be good allies to Wendell? Or people who want to be good allies in general, validated by the fact that it works for Wendell specifically?

These are two different decks. The first is a gift deck. The second is a general deck. The interview was about Wendell. The extraction question has to be answered before card writing proceeds.

## 🌊 Shaman — What's Moving Below the Surface

**What I see:** The interview was apparently about listening mechanics and capacity matching. But underneath, something much more specific was being mapped: the specific ways the dominant allyship culture has harmed Wendell. The Shaman's job is to name what's underneath.

**What's actually being mapped:**

1. **The emotional labor economy.** Wendell has been performing emotional labor for people who were supposed to be helping him. The "how can I help?" question requires Wendell to do the work of figuring out what help looks like — work he's not being compensated for, work he didn't sign up for. The deck, if it works, restores the emotional labor economy: the helper does the work of knowing their capacity, not the person being helped.

2. **The customer service trap.** "My customer service skills tell me this isn't a powerful question." Wendell has been watching people perform helpfulness his whole life. He knows what competent service looks like — and he knows the difference between that and genuine help. The deck will be read against his customer service calibration. Cards that feel like service rather than help will fail the same test.

3. **The depletion pattern.** "The person who can't resource themselves can't sustain showing up. Your depletion is their loss." This is the structural consequence of the emotional labor economy. The deck's job is not just to make the allyship moment better. It's to make the depletion less. Every card should pass the test: does this card move energy toward or away from depletion?

4. **The co-creator identity.** "I would rather do all my work with the help of other people." Wendell doesn't experience asking for help the way most people experience it. He experiences it as team formation. The deck's frame should match that identity — not rescue, not charity, team. People who read this deck should feel invited into something, not corrective of something.

5. **The power dynamic that allyship culture reproduces.** When someone asks "how can I help?" from a position of not knowing their capacity, they're reproducing the power dynamic where the person with resources (the helper) gets to decide how they're deployed. Wendell has been managed, directed, and deployed by people who thought they were helping. The deck's job is to invert this: the person who wants to help comes with their capacity already verified, their offer already specific, their follow-through already committed.

**Shaman's observation:**

> The deck is not teaching people to be better allies. It's teaching them to stop being unconscious extractors of Wendell's emotional labor. That's a harder ask. It requires the reader to admit that their desire to help has been, in part, a form of taking. Not always. But sometimes. The deck has to hold that without shaming people out of trying.

## 📖 Sage — What This Reveals About the Universal Pattern

**What I see:** The interview was specific to Wendell. But certain things he said point to patterns that, once seen, cannot be unseen. The Sage's job is to extract what generalizes without losing the specificity that makes it true.

**What the interview revealed that applies beyond this deck:**

1. **The blank-check problem is universal.** "How can I help?" is not Wendell's specific trigger. It's a culturally reinforced failure mode. The blank check puts direction on the person who needs help. This is true in healthcare, education, social work, community organizing, and friendship. The deck that teaches people to come with specific offers rather than open questions is solving a problem bigger than allyship to Wendell.

2. **Listening is the skill that nobody teaches because it requires the listener to give up status.** The reason "how can I help?" dominates is that it lets the helper keep their status. Asking questions makes you the one who doesn't know. Reflecting back makes you the one who might have heard wrong. "Tell me more" makes you the one who hasn't figured it out yet. Good listening is a status surrender that most allyship training never names, let alone teaches. The deck that teaches it is teaching something that the dominant culture structurally discourages.

3. **The helper's feelings are the biggest barrier to good helping.** "They're afraid of looking foolish." "Their story is more important than the other person's lived reality." "They want to feel powerful in the helper position." These aren't Wendell-specific observations. They're the reason most help fails to help. The deck that teaches people to notice their own emotional process while helping — without dissociating from it — is teaching the skill that distinguishes actual helpers from performed helpers.

4. **The completion signal belongs to the person being helped.** Most helping is structured around the helper's comfort level: the conversation ends when the helper is done processing. The interview revealed this clearly: "until I was done" — not until they were comfortable. This is the single most counterintuitive thing the deck will teach, because it requires the helper to give up control of the interaction. That's a high-level relational skill that most people have never been asked to practice.

5. **Co-creation is the correct frame for sustainable allyship.** The person who wants to rescue will burn out. The person who wants to co-create has a team. The interview named this explicitly: "I would rather do all my work with the help of other people." The deck that names this frame and gives people tools for co-creation is building something that outlasts any individual interaction.

**Sage's meta-observation:**

> Every allyship failure mode named in this interview has the same root: the helper's need for their own outcome. They need to feel like they helped. They need to have offered something. They need the interaction to resolve in a way that lets them feel good about it. The deck that teaches people to want the other person's outcome more than their own is teaching the thing that all the skills depend on.

**The design question this generates:**

> Can a card deck teach someone to want the other person's outcome more than their own? Or does that require something the deck can't produce — a shift in character that comes from somewhere else?

If the deck can't produce that shift, what CAN it produce? And is that enough?

## Canonical Corrections (Sage Verified)

The following items were stated as uncertain in the analysis. They now have canonical answers from the MTGOA framework:

**1. The 6 self-sabotaging beliefs** — confirmed from Chapter 2 (Controller gate):
> I'm not good enough. / I'm not ready. / I don't belong. / I'm insignificant. / I'm not worthy. / I'm not capable.

These are the blocking material for ♦️ Clean Up. The deck does not need to invent or discover these — they are the canonical Controller beliefs that produce self-sabotage.

**2. The four allyship domains** — confirmed from the BAR system schema:
> GATHERING_RESOURCES | SKILLFUL_ORGANIZING | DIRECT_ACTION | RAISE_AWARENESS

These are the triage structure. The deck's triage mechanic maps to these domains. Note: these are SEPARATE from the Kotter-based suit taxonomy. Two taxonomies confirmed — they don't compete, they layer. The suit determines the TYPE of move. The domain determines the AREA of impact.

**3. EA energy signatures** — "fire and peace" was Wendell's personal calibration, not a universal constant. The canonical EA system (Metal/Fear, Fire/Anger, Water/Sadness, Wood/Joy, Earth/Neutrality) gives a framework for calibrating ally energy to the specific person's needs. Wendell wants anger + neutrality. Another person might want joy + fear. The deck should teach the calibration skill, not imprint Wendell's preferences as universal.

**4. Practice loop for listening** — confirmed: get in front of people you want to help. Practice is relational, not solitary. The deck's practice structure should be: draw a card → apply in a real interaction → notice what happened.

## The Question That Survived All Six Faces

**Can a card deck teach someone to want the other person's outcome more than their own?**

Answered by the Sage: yes — but not through argument. Through structure. Every card in the deck is structured to redirect attention from "what do I get out of this" to "what does the other person need." The person who practices the cards repeatedly is practicing a redirection of attention that, over time, becomes disposition. This is how habits form. The deck is a practice loop, not a persuasion device.

What the deck CAN produce: the habit of wanting the other person's outcome. What it cannot produce alone: the initial choice to pick up the deck. That requires something else — a person who already wants to show up differently and is looking for the practice structure to do it.

**Design implication:** The deck should assume the reader already wants to show up better. It is not converting anyone. It is providing practice infrastructure for people who are already trying.
