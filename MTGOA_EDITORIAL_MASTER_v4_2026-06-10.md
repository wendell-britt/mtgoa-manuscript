## What this rebuild is

The manuscript is draft-complete but built on the wrong structure. Each chapter currently walks all eight gate-selves (the repeated full-walk), the Effective Allyship Formula exists in no chapter, and the character sheet is front-loaded and never returned to. The rebuild fixes the architecture, not the sentences.

The book is one journey: **the reader arrives as the Villager and reaches the center of the self as the Player.** The eight gates are the descent. Each chapter's Face engages one gate-self, delivers two epiphanies (Face teaching + gate opening), and writes one entry on the character sheet. By the end, the sheet *is* the Formula applied in the reader's own words — and the book's thesis has escalated from "allyship is a game" to "allyship *needs* to be gamified, or people can't actually get better."

## The locked spine

### The macro journey
Villager → Player. The reader arrives as the Villager — aspiring ally, Protector-run, gathering information to stay safe from harm and cancellation. Reading-as-armor is the stay-stuck loop and the first gate, named as the same thing. The book descends through eight gates to the Vulnerable Child at the center, who is finally ready to play — and who can now use the other seven voices as allies to ally with others. - **Ch7–Ch8 earn it:** allyship doesn't just benefit from being a game — it *needs* to be gamified, because a game is the only structure that lets people actually get better. This is the book's major epiphany.

The reader selects ONE belief at the Ch0 character sheet. That belief is her campaign — the spine of this entire read. This is what makes the book re-readable: one campaign per belief, six campaigns deep.

The six beliefs: *I'm not ready. I'm not capable. I'm not worthy. I'm not good enough. I'm insignificant. I don't belong.*

### The eight gates — chapter, Face, gate-self, gate epiphany, Face epiphany

**Ch1 — Shaman / Protector (the Villager's gate)**
- *Gate epiphany:* The Protector keeps the body alive; most people only show up for shared threats. Allyship starts with protecting yourself — you can't protect others from a body that's never felt protected. The protection you're running *right now* (reading-as-armor) is the Protector's strategy, not a flaw, but protection eventually has to become something you *do*, not something you *know.*
- *Golden shadow note:* the Protector also buries golden shadow — our best selves, our passions — out of guilt, shame, and fear of ridicule. Forward-referenced in Ch0; taught here.
- *Face epiphany:* allyship is relational, consent-based, trust-earned — and your somatic intelligence is yours to use.

**Ch2 — Shaman / Controller**
- *Gate epiphany:* The Controller sets the standard — once you're alive, it tells you *how* to live. It's your emotions that control and set the standard of what allyship moves you to action. The Controller's standard can be updated.
- *Face epiphany:* identify your superpower through emotional literacy — traced from your chosen belief (**Formula step 1**).

**Ch3 — Challenger / Skeptic**
- *Gate epiphany:* The Skeptic doubts. Turn that doubt onto narratives of allyship not serving real emotional satisfaction — yours and the people your superpower would serve. Skeptic → wonder is the epiphany.
- *Face epiphany:* find where your superpower should actually be applied (**Formula step 2**).

**Ch4 — Regent / Fixer-Healer**
- *Gate epiphany:* The Fixer knows what to hold and what to repair — but works at the whim of whoever the Regent of allyship is. Who have you been treating as the authority, and whose emotional satisfaction have you actually been fighting for?
- *Face epiphany:* roles, responsibilities, and chosen authority make return easier (**Formula step 3 begins**).

**Ch5 — Architect / Emotional Body**
- *Gate epiphany:* The Emotional Body is where alchemy gets specific. Emotional signals point to values; you can build systems that support those values.
- *Face epiphany:* leverage and environment design make the right thing easier (**Formula steps 3–4**).

**Ch6 — Diplomat / Victim** (the heaviest critique)
- *Gate epiphany:* Diplomats uplift victims as advocates and collapse into victimhood themselves; they're the storytellers. What stories are you telling, about whom, and do they *directly* improve the well-being of those involved?
- *Face epiphany:* honest terms, mutual sustainability, show up consistently (**Formula step 4**).

**Ch7 — Sage / Damaged Self**
- *Gate epiphany:* The Damaged Self has transcended the story and can look at the damage — and in looking, sees what's survivable. You will never protect anyone from complete harm — and well-being doesn't mean unharmed.
- *Face epiphany:* see the whole board without hiding above it; use the Formula wisely from altitude. **The macro thesis lands: gamification isn't optional.**

**Ch8 — Player / Vulnerable Child** (the center)
- *Gate epiphany:* Ready to play in earnest. All the other voices have been protecting it all along; it can now use its gates and Faces as allies to ally with others.
- *Face epiphany:* turn the whole thing into lived practice; the full sheet assembled. **Macro thesis completes; the reader returns to the village as a Player.**

### The seven-strand reverse-outline template

For each chapter, the outline checks seven strands. Strands 1–4 operate at the chapter level. Strand 5 (the micro-bridge) operates at the *section* level. Strands 6–7 are the structural operations.

**Strand 1 — Book-bridge position**
What epiphany milestone is this on the Villager→Player arc, and does the chapter deliver it? The book-level bridge runs: backstory (Ch0 confession) → desire (reader's private question) → wall (the debt/bargain) → conflict (the impossible bind) → epiphany (allyship needs the game) → transformation (each Face chapter) → achievement (Player, Ch8). Each chapter is one beat in the transformation arc.

**Strand 2 — Chapter-internal bridge**
Do the chapter's major sections move through the eight bridge beats as a chapter-level arc?
- Backstory → Internal desire → External desire → Wall → Conflict → Epiphany → Transformation → Achievement
Which beats are present, weak, or missing? The chapter's *epiphany* beat is the Face teaching (the Formula step). The *transformation* beat is the gate engagement. The *achievement* beat resolves on two axes (see Strand 5).

**Strand 3 — Author-arc touchdown**
Where does Wendell's own relationship to allyship show in this chapter? The trust-earning thread runs across the whole book — each chapter has at least one place where the author's lived experience mirrors the reader's gate, without manufacturing one that isn't true.

**Strand 4 — Epiphany↔practice coupling**
Is every epiphany proved by a tool, practice, or bounded action? No orphaned insights. The First Move (WB-1) is the chapter's external action — time-boxed, involves another person, not satisfiable by reflection or a BAR alone.

**Strand 5 — Per-section micro-bridge (the EA layer)**
Each section within the chapter is its own micro-bridge. The outline maps:
- **Charged emotion** at the section's desire beat — the specific EA channel in its dissatisfied register (e.g., *anxious fear*, not just *fear*) that the section opens in the reader
- **Satisfied state** at the achievement beat — the same channel metabolized (fear → excitement; anger → triumph; sadness → poignance; joy → bliss; neutrality → peace)
- **External artifact** at the achievement beat — the concrete thing the reader makes or names (a sheet entry, a named belief, a captured BAR, a chosen situation, a held question)

The achievement beat resolves on *both* axes: internal (the emotion metabolized) and external (the artifact produced). This is the double-resolution that makes sections feel complete rather than accumulative.

**For Ch2 specifically:** the EA micro-bridges must retroactively name emotions the reader has *already moved through* in Ch0/Ch1 — pulling from the felt corpus planted there. The mechanics land on lived experience, not abstraction. Known corpus from Ch0/Ch1 passes:
- Ch0 Confession: *anger → triumph* (the madness that drew the line)
- Ch0 Private Question: *fear → excitement* (the flinch that became the live wire)
- Ch0 Which Game/Passion: *fear of ridicule → excitement of golden shadow*
- Ch0 Game Master: *anger → triumph* (agency reclaimed)
- Ch1 Protector: *fear → excitement* (reading-as-armor → the relief of being seen)

**Refinement pass (deeper drafts, not first build):** For each section, diagnose the *specific dissatisfied register* the ideal reader is likely experiencing (anxious vs. numb fear; frustrated vs. defeated anger; despairing vs. apathetic sadness) and tune the section so the transformation delivers the payoff appropriate to that exact state — not a generic version of the channel.

**Strand 6 — Character-sheet entry**
Does the chapter's practice write its assigned sheet line (per the table above)? The entry should feel like *arrival*, not an assignment.

**Strand 7 — Gate engagement**
(a) Is the eight-gate walk removed?
(b) Is there one section where the Face engages this chapter's one gate-self?
(c) Does it deliver the gate epiphany (per the spine)?
(d) Is the ask *deeper* than the prior chapter's gate? (The descent deepens chapter to chapter.)

# SOMATIC LANGUAGE PASS — logged during Ch2 build

## The problem
The manuscript's somatic language currently *tells* the reader what she feels rather than *inviting* her to notice what she actually feels. Examples of the failure mode:

- "The emotion would arrive — theirs and mine — and my chest would do this particular thing: a tightening, a pulling inward, like a door closing fast." — This is the author's somatic experience being narrated to the reader. It's specific and true for Wendell. It becomes prescriptive for the reader if she doesn't feel it that way.
- "You feel the impulse to act — anger rising, a line to draw..." — Assigning a specific emotion (anger) to a moment the reader may be experiencing differently.
- Gate prompts that say "you feel X" rather than "notice what's here."

## The distinction
**Telling:** "Your chest tightens. Fear arrives. You feel the grip."
**Inviting:** "Something arrives — maybe in the chest, maybe somewhere else. Notice where it lands in your body. Whatever it is, it has information."

The difference is the difference between the author's somatic map and the reader's actual body. The author's specificity is useful as one *example* ("for me, it was a tightening in the chest") but harmful if it becomes the only legitimate somatic experience.

## The fix (Voice Pass 3 — run after structural rebuild)
Across all chapters, audit every somatic description for the telling/inviting distinction:
- Author's own body: keep specific, own it explicitly ("for me it was...")
- Reader's body: shift to inviting language ("notice where this lands," "something may arrive in the body — not necessarily where you expect it," "feel for the signal, whatever form it takes")
- Avoid prescribing specific body locations to the reader (chest, throat, jaw) unless giving multiple options or framing them as one possibility
- Gate prompts especially: each gate prompt should invite the reader's own body read, not confirm a pre-set feeling

## Why this matters for Ch2 specifically
Ch2 is the EA teaching chapter — it's where the reader learns to trust her *own* somatic signals. If the chapter tells her what she's supposed to feel, it undermines the exact trust it's trying to build. The Shaman's gift is the reader's body knowing first. The chapter has to model that by asking rather than telling.

**Sequencing:** This is a whole-book pass, run after the structural rebuild. Flag instances in Ch2 especially during the D3–D6 builds so the new prose doesn't add to the problem.

# VOICE FLAG — "You already know this in your body" and variants

## The problem
The phrase "you already know this in your body" (and close variants: "you know this already," "you've felt this," "you know this one") appears repeatedly across the manuscript. It functions as a rhetorical shortcut — signaling intimacy and shared knowledge without earning it. It's doing two things wrong simultaneously:

1. **Telling not inviting** — it asserts the reader has a specific felt experience she may not have. If she doesn't recognize it, she feels excluded rather than seen.
2. **Flattery as substitute for earning** — it creates the *feeling* of being understood without actually delivering the understanding. The knowing nod rather than the real connection.

## The fix (flag for somatic language pass — Voice Pass 3)
Find and evaluate every instance of:
- "You already know this in your body"
- "You know this one"
- "You've felt this before"
- "You know exactly what I mean"
- Any variant that presumes shared somatic knowledge

Replace with inviting language that opens a door rather than assumes the reader has already walked through it:
- "There may be a moment you can point to —" (lets her find it or not)
- "Notice whether there's a version of this in your own body" (genuinely inviting)
- "If you've felt this, you know what I mean. If you haven't yet, keep it nearby." (both/and)

## Scope
Whole-book pass, bundled with Voice Pass 3 (somatic language pass). Flag every instance; some will be legitimate (where the author is speaking from his own experience and the "you" is genuinely earned); most will need reworking.

# PEDAGOGICAL LAYER — Teaching design pass (post-structural-rebuild)

This is a distinct pass from the structural rebuild and the voice passes. It addresses how the book *teaches* rather than what it says. Run after the structural rebuild is complete, before copyedit.

## 1. Teaching notes on EA, WAVE, and app/appendix connections

Each chapter that introduces or uses EA/WAVE mechanics needs:
- A brief teaching note explaining *what the reader is being asked to do* and *why* before the mechanics are given (not the whole explanation — the framing that makes the mechanic legible)
- An explicit pointer to the appendix for the full technique instructions, so the chapter prose stays lean
- An explicit pointer to the app for the guided practice version — the app holds the depth; the book plants the logic
- A "wax on wax off" framing: the reader isn't expected to master the mechanic before moving on. She's expected to make one contact with it. Mastery comes through repetition across campaigns, not through completing the chapter.

## 2. Brainstorming practices per chapter

Two formal brainstorming practices are needed — one per Shaman chapter, both connected to the app:

**Practice A — Superpower brainstorm (Ch2):** A structured practice for finding your superpower when the belief→superpower trace isn't immediately obvious. The reader needs a way to generate candidates, not just accept the one the table suggests. Should surface: what she does naturally that others find hard, where she gets praised for things that feel effortless, what she was forced to develop that others weren't. Format: guided prompts → app capture → pattern recognition. This is distinct from the quiz (which gives an answer); this is the practice of generating candidates through reflection.

**Practice B — Emotion-to-information (Ch2, also useful in Ch5/Architect):** A structured practice for understanding what a specific emotion is trying to tell you. Not "feel the emotion," which the somatic pass will address — but "once you've noticed the signal, how do you read what it's carrying?" The WAVE-Spiral teaches the mechanics of metabolizing; this practice teaches the *reading* — what does this specific anger/fear/sadness/joy mean in this specific situation? Format: situation named → channel identified → the question the emotion is asking → the information it's carrying. Connected to the app's BAR capture.

## 3. WAVE-somatic explanation

The chapter currently teaches WAVE as a conceptual framework without fully explaining what the somatic experience of running it actually is. Readers need to know:
- What does it feel like *in the body* to run a WAVE stage? (Without prescribing — inviting)
- What's the difference between WAVE-Somatic (the full body-based version) and a lighter cognitive version?
- When is each appropriate?

Point to Appendix D for the full WAVE-Somatic technique instructions. The chapter carries the concept; the appendix carries the method.

## 4. The Show move as allyship progress engine

# ARCHITECTURAL PRINCIPLE — Face vs. Gate register separation

## The problem
The Gates and the Faces are starting to sound like the same voice. In Ch3 especially, the Skeptic gate (internal) and the Challenger concept (external) are both about doubt and discernment, and the content adjacency is causing the registers to merge. A reader could mistake the Skeptic gate for the Challenger teaching and vice versa.

## The distinction
**The Face** is the external capacity — how the reader shows up in the world, the allyship move, what other people experience. Challenger draws lines. Shaman reads the room. Diplomat holds honest terms. These are visible, relational, aimed outward.

**The Gate** is the internal voice — the part of the self that must be met before the external capacity can deploy cleanly. The gate runs underneath the surface before the Face speaks. Controller governs the emotional standard. Skeptic doubts the self's own reads. Protector keeps the body safe. These are internal, protective, operating prior to action.

## The register fix
Face sections speak *about the external game* — how the capacity works in relationships, situations, and allyship moves. Register: descriptive, aimed outward at the world.

Gate sections speak *to the internal voice directly* — addressing the part of the reader that's been running this pattern, meeting it in its own logic, updating its standard. Register: intimate, aimed inward at the self, often in the voice of the gate itself or the author's own experience of meeting it.

Same second-person address in both, but different orientation:
- Face: "here's how the Challenger operates in a room with a real person"
- Gate: "here's the voice inside you that keeps the Challenger from running clean, and here's how you meet it"

## Structural signal
A useful heuristic: if the content could appear in a section called "how to use this in allyship" it belongs in the Face teaching. If the content would feel more at home in a section called "what's been running underneath your allyship" it belongs in the Gate.

## How Ch2 got it right
The Controller gate (internal — what's been governing the emotional standard) is categorically distinct from the Shaman teaching (external — how to read and metabolize emotion in allyship). They share the chapter but speak from different registers. The sequencing also helps: gate comes *before* the mechanics teaching, so the reader meets the internal voice before she receives the external tools.

## Apply going forward
- Each chapter: gate section and Face teaching should be auditable as distinct registers
- If the gate section reads like an extension of the Face teaching, it needs its register pulled back inward
- The gate section should address the reader's inner voice; the Face section should address the reader's outer behavior
- Ch3 specifically: the Skeptic gate (internal doubt-voice, aimed at self) vs. the Challenger teaching (external discernment, aimed at situations and people) — these need distinct tones even though both involve doubt

# CH6 DESIGN — Victim gate full epiphany (Drama Triangle)

## The gate's specific distortion
Using victimhood as plot armor. The story gets protected more than the people who need help. Two directions:
1. **Telling your victim story to get sympathy** — centers you, not the person who needs help
2. **Giving sympathy to someone else's victim story** — keeps them in the story, not liberated from it

Neither is empowering. Both are the drama triangle running.

## The drama triangle (Karpman)
Victim → Villain → Savior. All three roles are disempowering:
- The Victim stays small to get protection
- The Villain gets assigned responsibility so others don't have to take it
- The Savior is the Victim with more resources — rescuing to feel needed, not to liberate

Effective allyship refuses the triangle. The Savior is not better than the Victim. The whole structure keeps everyone stuck.

## The replacement structure: Challenger, Ally, Hero
- **Challenger** — names what's true (the Challenger's gift, Ch3)
- **Ally** — supports the work without centering themselves
- **Hero** — the person whose liberation is the point; the agent of their own story, not the recipient of sympathy

The person you're trying to help is the Hero. Your job is not to rescue them. Your job is to help them become the Hero of their own story. That's the difference between allyship and saviorism.

## The Victim gate's mechanism in the Diplomat chapter
The Diplomat's distortion is frozen presence — but the mechanism is the drama triangle:
- Staying because leaving would make you the Villain
- Staying to position yourself as the Savior
- Staying because you've adopted the Victim role ("they need me, I can't leave")
- The Controller uses victimhood to maintain "feel bad to do good" — suffering proves seriousness
- The Protector uses it as armor — "I'm too wounded to be challenged"

## The gate's epiphany
**The story is plot armor.** The moment you use victimhood (yours or someone else's) to protect a narrative instead of to liberate a person, you've left allyship and entered the drama triangle.

The empowered move: step out of the triangle. Bring people with you. Ally with them in becoming the Hero, not protecting the Victim.

## The effective allyship move
You can bring people together to help you help others — which means liberating them from the story as well. Not "I will save you from your victimhood" (Savior). "Let me show you a different triangle, and let's build something there together." The Diplomat at full capacity doesn't hold the drama triangle's field. The Diplomat creates conditions for people to step out of it.

## Formula step 4 connection
You can only show up consistently (Formula step 4) if your presence isn't running on the drama triangle's fuel. Sympathy-based presence burns out. Empowerment-based presence is renewable. The Victim gate is what stands between the reader and sustainable presence.

## Source note
Drama Triangle: Karpman (1968). The replacement (Challenger/Ally/Hero) is MTGOA's specific contribution — the empowered triangle that allyship is supposed to create.
