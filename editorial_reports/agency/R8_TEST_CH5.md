# R8 stress test — Chapter 5

## Verdict

**Break rate: 6 of 25 sites tested (24%), but the number hides two very different stories.**

- **Village-fable scope (R8's intended target): 2 breaks + 1 flagged keep-candidate out of 21 sites (~9.5% break rate).** Within its actual scope, R8 mostly works, exactly as the GAP proposal claims. But "mostly" requires an editor to hand-check every instance for two silent failure modes rhythm and syllable counts will never surface (see below) — this is not a mechanical find-and-replace.
- **Office/role scope (the pattern R8 was never meant to cover): 4 of 4 breaks (100%).** Confirmed: R8 does not extend there. See below.

**Is R8 safe to adopt for this chapter?** Safe as a *targeted* op with a tight scope gate (village-as-fictional-population only, checked instance-by-instance), not safe as a blanket "pluralize the collective noun" macro. Two of its failures in this chapter are invisible to a syllable-count check, which is the exact instrument the anchor tells editors to trust.

## Does R8 extend to the office/role pattern?

**No. 0 of 4 tested sites survive as a pure number change.** The reason is structural, not incidental: "the village" is (per the GAP proposal's own claim) a noun that already denotes a population — pluralizing just unpacks members that were always implied. "Loyalty," "leadership," "organizations," and "patriarch" are not population nouns in that sense; none of them contain a hidden set of persons that a suffix reveals.

- **Line 315 — `Real loyalty sees… and decides…`.** "Loyalty" is a quality/mass noun. "Real loyalties see the whole inheritance" does not produce persons — it produces more abstractions (multiple instances of the quality), which still can't see or decide. There is no population hiding inside "loyalty" for R8 to unpack. This site needs R1 or nothing; the ledger already flags R1 here as a keep-candidate because it costs the four-sentence anaphora. R8 offers no third option — it simply doesn't apply.
- **Line 628 — `New leadership wants…`.** The ledger's own accepted fix is "the new leaders want…" — which *looks* like R8 (ends up plural) but isn't: "leadership" (abstract mass noun) → "leaders" (concrete count noun) is a different word, not a suffix on the same root. Try the actual R8 operation — add a plural morpheme to "leadership" — and you get "leaderships," which is not idiomatic English for a group of people. What fixed this site was R1's "swap the abstraction for a concrete plural noun in the same slot," not R8's "pluralize the collective."
- **Line 187 — `the organizations that used tradition to protect power`.** Already plural, and the defect survives its own plurality: "organizations" denotes institutional entities, not a population synonym. Unlike "the village," "organizations" ≠ "the people who ran them" — you cannot get from one to the other by changing number, only by changing the noun. The ledger's accepted fix, "the people who used tradition…," is R1, not R8, for the same reason.
- **Line 297 (new miss, not in the ledger) — `a patriarch does its own kind of damage`.** "The ones who wanted the crown were looking for a patriarch to give themselves to, and a patriarch does its own kind of damage." Pluralizing ("patriarchs do their own kind of damage") does something worse than just fail — it changes the target. The original is a structural claim about a *role* (whoever occupies this position will do this kind of damage). The plural reads as a claim about a *class of people* who happen to hold that role — which is exactly the reversal the book's own Jerk-archetype targeting rule forbids: mock the process, never the person. R8 here doesn't just fail to fix the site, it actively risks turning a process-critique into a person-critique.

**Conclusion: R8 has zero purchase on the office/role register. That register needs its own op** — most likely R1's "swap the abstraction for a concrete plural noun" (already in use at 628/187) as one lane, and a "name the role's structural logic, not its occupants" move for cases like 297 where even a concrete-noun swap would mis-target.

## Are the Faces Grade 2 or Grade 6?

**This reads closer to Grade 2 than the ledger allowed, but it is genuinely contested — a registry question, not a repair question. I am not ruling it.**

Evidence for Grade 2 (licensed, same as anchor 4):
- Anchor 4 is *literally this chapter* — `ch5:516`, the Mr. Inadequate / Fixer-Healer passage at line 548 ("I have a part of me I call Mr. Inadequate… I have watched myself hand him to the Fixer. **The Fixer is happy to take him.**"). `GAP_VOICE_ANCHOR.md`'s own table rules this mechanism agency-safe: "named part — Grade 2, licensed, mechanism in canon." 
- The Regent, Challenger, and Architect are drawn from the *same canonical daemon-naming system* as the Fixer, the Protector, the Controller, and the Skeptic — all of which appear elsewhere in this same chapter (line 522: "The Protector decides whether you go in. The Controller decides how you may behave… The Skeptic decides whether what you react to was ever real.") as uncontested, licensed Grade 2 parts taking intention verbs. The fable's Regent/Challenger/Architect run the identical grammar: a named part, holding perception/intention/social-causal verbs, across an extended passage. Structurally, Sections 1–2 read as the same device as the Mr. Inadequate passage, staged at greater length as a myth rather than a paragraph.

Evidence against (why the ledger called it neither Grade 1 nor "a canonical Grade 2 part"):
- Every other Grade 2 use in the book, including anchor 4 itself, is explicitly framed as **interior to one named person** — "I have a part of me I call…" The Regent/Challenger/Architect are not framed as parts of any single "I." They act *upon* an external population (the village) at social scale, arrive and depart from outside it, and hold entire biographical arcs (the Regent builds walls, makes a mistake, argues with the Architect, is exiled) that read less like an internal daemon and more like a fully external character — closer to the office/role pattern's territory (an actor with institutional-scale effects) than to a psychological part.

**The crux for Wendell:** is "a part of me" load-bearing for the Grade 2 license, or is the *naming convention itself* (a capitalized, canon-listed daemon name drawn from the book's fixed roster) sufficient regardless of whether the part belongs to one person or is staged as acting on a population? The chapter answers this differently depending which passage you read. R8 has nothing to say either way — pluralizing "the Regent" is not coherent in any reading (there is one Regent in the fable, with one biography; "the Regents" either means nothing or gestures at a different, undeveloped claim). This is not an R8 site. It is a licensing question upstream of any repair.

## Where R8 breaks

Four distinct failure shapes surfaced in this chapter, only one of which the GAP proposal names:

1. **Group acting on itself** (the shape the proposal names, via the ch4 "it taught its people" example). No live instance of this exact shape appears in ch5 — the closest, line 717's "the people inside it," resolves benignly rather than breaking. Its absence here doesn't mean it's rare; it means this chapter's failures are elsewhere.
2. **Place/vehicle mismatch** (line 171). "The village" is doing double duty as population-noun and place-noun/metaphor-vehicle in the same sentence. Pluralizing serves the population reading and destroys the place reading. Rhythm is unaffected — a syllable check passes this straight through.
3. **Possessive semantic drift** (line 70ii). Pluralizing a possessive ("the village's boundaries" → "the villagers' boundaries") is grammatical and syllable-neutral, but silently swaps "the community's shared norms" for "individuals' personal boundaries" — two different concepts the book treats separately elsewhere. This is the most dangerous failure mode found: nothing about the sentence signals that anything changed.
4. **Process-critique becomes person-critique** (line 297, office register). Pluralizing an office/role noun ("a patriarch" → "patriarchs") can convert a claim about a structural role into a claim about the people who hold it — which the book's own humor rules explicitly forbid.

None of shapes 2–4 are mentioned in `GAP_VOICE_ANCHOR.md`. The proposal's own worked table (ch4/ch3 examples) only demonstrates shape 1.

The office/role pattern fails wholesale (4/4): "loyalty," "leadership," "organizations," and "patriarch" are not population nouns the way "the village" is, so the pluralization mechanism has nothing to act on. Where a plural-looking fix exists (628, 187), it's actually R1's concrete-noun substitution wearing R8's clothes.
