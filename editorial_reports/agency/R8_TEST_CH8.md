# R8 stress test — Chapter 8

## Verdict

**Sentence-level break rate: 0 of 23 (0%).** Every literal "the village [verb]"
construction in this chapter survives mechanical pluralization — no site produces
nonsense, and the specific hard-failure shape the proposal itself warns about (a
village *possessing* its members, "the villagers taught their people") **does not
occur anywhere in ch8**. That shape belongs to ch3 and ch4's village, which acts on
its own population. Ch8's village acts toward an individual (the Sage, a named
person the village exiles) or toward abstractions (rules, games, a caricature) —
structurally different, and it is why this chapter's break rate is not what the
"one in five needs real work" estimate in the GAP doc would predict. Zero sites
here need R6 or compression-as-repair. R8 is not the wrong grammar for this
chapter.

**But R8 fails a test the sentence level cannot see.** Applied uniformly and
mechanically — which is what "a suffix pass" implies — R8 produces **23 instances
of "the villagers"** across one chapter, **12 of them inside a single 32-line
section**. That is not a grammar failure. It is the exact rhetorical failure this
whole audit exists to fix, restaged with a different noun: a drumbeat. R8 does not
carry this chapter at volume as a pure mechanical substitution. It carries this
chapter only if paired with the same subject-variation discipline the ledger
already had to invent for R1's 23 instances — which means R8 is not actually
cheaper than R1 in the one chapter where volume is the whole test. It is the same
amount of editorial judgment wearing a suffix.

**1 site (line 164) is flagged as a keep-candidate** — a triple-anaphora ("It
learned... It learned... It learned...") whose eerie, single-organism cadence is
plural-fragile. Not ruled; flagged for Wendell.

**1 shape (the liturgical lookalike, line 373 and its twin at line 631) is entirely
outside R8's reach** — "the pattern" is not a population, has no members, and
"the patterns show" is absurd. This needs its own routing rule, not R8. See below.

**Net finding: R8 is safe to adopt in ch8 at the sentence level, and unsafe to
adopt as a mechanical rule at the volume this chapter actually contains.** Scope
the amendment to require subject variation as a condition of the op, not an
afterthought, or the fix inherits the disease.

## The volume question

**Count: 23 literal instances of "the village" as a clause subject** across the
chapter (counting only sites carrying a perception/intention/social-causal/speech
verb; excludes the two section-heading instances at lines 56 and 136, which sit
outside the ledger's declared body-text scope, and excludes line 77's "the village
would pause," which is a pattern-class/mechanical verb the ledger correctly did not
flag). Mechanical R8 turns every one of them into "the villagers."

**Spacing — this is the finding.** They do not spread evenly:

- **12 of 23 (52%) fall in Section 1** (lines 73–105), a single 32-line, ~900-word
  fable. That is one new "the villagers" roughly every 75 words for three straight
  pages.
- Within that span, three separate spots have two-or-more occurrences within a
  handful of sentences: lines 95 ("The village did not know this... The village
  asked..."), lines 99 ("the village made its preference official... The village
  kept the caricature"), and the 152/154/156/158/160/162/164 run in Section 2,
  which stacks **seven** instances into 12 lines.
- The remaining instances (Section 4 line 525/529/541, Section 7 line 828) are
  spaced far enough apart (hundreds of lines) that each reads clean in isolation.

**Ruling: yes, the chapter needs subject variation, not one mechanical
substitution.** The ledger already proved this is fixable — it varied R1 four ways
across the same 23 sites (generic plural, the book's own "you" address, reordered
clause, swapped-in licensed noun) specifically because 23 identical subject-nouns
in one chapter reads as a tic regardless of which noun it is. R8 does not get an
exemption from that discipline just because the noun it repeats is grammatically
licensed. A licensed tic is still a tic.

**Proposed variation set**, matching the four moves the ledger already used for R1,
applied to R8's plural baseline instead of a rewrite:

1. **"the villagers"** — the default, but capped. Use it as the *first* mention in
   a run, not every mention.
2. **Pronoun continuation ("they"/"their"/"it")** — once "the villagers" has been
   established as antecedent in a paragraph, subsequent verbs in the same
   paragraph should ride the pronoun rather than reintroduce the noun. Lines 87,
   95, 105, 158, 164 already do this partially (the prose's own "they'd heard" at
   line 87 shows the author's ear already reaching for the plural pronoun before
   any edit — see the line 87 site note below); R8 should finish what the prose
   already started rather than overwrite it with a fresh "the villagers" at every
   verb.
3. **"people there" / "everyone at the table" / "the room"** — swap-in nouns
   already licensed and already used elsewhere in this exact chapter's own R1
   proposals (lines 77, 87, 158, 160, 529). These are free to reuse for R8 runs
   too; nothing stops "the villagers" from alternating with "people there" inside
   the same section.
4. **"no one" / "nobody"** — for the negation-class sites (line 105's "never
   meant," "never said"), a negative-quantifier subject is both licensed and often
   sharper than "the villagers never..." ("No one meant for that to happen" reads
   cleaner than "The villagers never meant for that to happen").

Applied with this rotation, Section 1's 12 instances could run something like:
*villagers → they → they → villagers (new paragraph) → they → people at the table
→ villagers → no one → they* — never two "the villagers" in adjacent sentences.
That is an edit, not a suffix pass. It costs the same editorial attention R1 cost.
**The "cheaper than R1" claim holds only if variation is optional. It is not
optional at this volume.**

## Cumulative rhythm cost

Counting only the changed span ("the village" → "the villagers" = 3 → 4
syllables, +1 each) across all 23 instances, then subtracting the compression
moves a competent editor would make in the same stroke (contractions absorbing the
added syllable: "doesn't" → "don't" at lines 525 and 541; "loses" → "lose" at line
160; "had made" → "had" at line 89, matching the task's own worked example):

- **Raw cost, no compensating edits: +23 syllables.**
- **Cost as competently applied (folding in free contractions the plural
  licenses): +19 syllables**, across roughly 8,400 words of chapter body. That is
  not a rhythm problem in aggregate — it is well under a tenth of one percent of
  the chapter's total syllable count.
- **But it is not evenly distributed.** Roughly **+12 of those 19 syllables land
  inside Section 1's 900 words** — the fable the ledger's own bloc note already
  flagged as trading its "fairy-tale cadence" for something slower under R1. R8
  costs that section real weight even though it costs the chapter almost nothing.

**Anchor 5 itself is untouched.** "The triumph is going down and coming back
without feeling diminished by the trip. The view from above was the easy half."
(VOICE_ANCHOR.md cites this as ch8:342; in the current manuscript file the
passage sits at line 367 — a line-number drift between the spec and the current
draft, not a defect in this test.) No village-subject construction appears in or
near that passage; R8 never reaches it. **Compression as argument survives R8 at
the anchor site with zero cost, because R8 never touches that sentence.** The risk
anchor 5 actually flags — general compression discipline across the chapter — is
where the +12-syllable concentration in Section 1 does register, even though the
seven-word anchor sentence itself is safe.

## The liturgical lookalike (line 373) — what that shape needs

**Current:** > The pattern shows fully and nothing in you moves about it.

**R8 does not apply here, and cannot be made to apply.** R8's entire mechanism is
a number change: a fictional population's grammatical number is toggled because
its members are persons. "The pattern" has no members. It is not a population; it
is a single abstraction. "The patterns show fully" is not a repair, it's nonsense
in a different key — multiple patterns now inexplicably jointly perceiving
something, which is a worse violation than the original, not a better one, and it
also silently changes the claim from "one pattern, fully seen" to "several
patterns," which isn't in the text at all. There is no plural fix for a singleton.

**This confirms the shape is real and needs its own routing rule**, separate from
both R1 (the audit's default) and R8 (this proposal): a subject that mimics the
book's protected five-channel liturgy cadence — "[Subject] shows you what
matters," modeled on "Fear shows you what matters" — but whose subject is not one
of the five channels (Fear, Anger, Neutrality, Joy, Sadness) has no license and no
population to pluralize into. The ledger's own R1 fix ("You see it fully, and
nothing in you moves about it") is the only shape that works, because it does
what R8 cannot: it puts a perceiver back in the sentence rather than adjusting the
grammatical number of a subject that was never a population to begin with. The
twin at line 631 ("that is where the pattern shows") confirms this is a recurring
tic in the Diagnostician material specifically, not a one-off — both instances sit
in Sage-register passages about the Diagnostician mode, suggesting the liturgical
cadence is something this Face's own prose reaches for reflexively.

**Recommendation for the registry:** any site matching the "[Subject] shows /
tells / shows you what" liturgical pattern should be checked against the five
named channels before any op is chosen. If the subject is not one of the five, it
routes straight to R1 — R8 should never be offered as a candidate op for this
shape, because it has no purchase on it. This is a different gap than the one
GAP_VOICE_ANCHOR.md diagnoses (which is about the anchor set protecting effects
over mechanisms); this is a routing gap in the op-selection logic itself, and it's
worth a rule of its own so a future automated pass doesn't waste a cycle trying to
pluralize an abstraction that was never a population.

## Where R8 breaks

Nothing in ch8 breaks at the sentence level. What breaks is upstream and
downstream of the sentence:

1. **Volume, not grammar.** 23 instances, 12 of them inside one 32-line section,
   is a tic if applied as pure mechanical substitution. See "The volume question"
   above. This is the chapter's real failure mode, and it is a rhetorical failure
   of exactly the kind this whole audit exists to prevent — R8 solves the
   licensing problem and reopens the repetition problem in the same motion unless
   variation is mandatory.

2. **The worked example is not a pure number change.** Line 89, R8's own flagship
   site, quietly downgrades "had made" to "had" — a verb change, not a number
   change — and in doing so erases the one thing the ledger's reading identified
   as load-bearing in that exact sentence (rule-making as an active, legislative
   act, the chapter's clearest violation of its own thesis). The strict
   number-change-only version ("the villagers had made a rule") works fine
   grammatically and costs one syllable; using it instead of the compressed
   version preserves more of what the sentence is actually doing.

3. **One site is agency-clean but voice-fragile.** Line 164's triple "It
   learned..." anaphora survives R8 mechanically but may be doing deliberate
   single-organism dread-work that "They learned..." dilutes. Flagged, not
   ruled — see the site entry above.

4. **The liturgical lookalike is untouched, not broken by R8 — worse, unreachable
   by it.** Lines 373 and 631 need R1, always, because there is no population
   there to pluralize. This isn't a failure of R8's execution; it's proof R8 has
   a narrow, correctly narrow scope, and the registry needs a routing rule that
   keeps it from being tried where it cannot work.

No site in this chapter demonstrates the "group acting on itself" failure the
GAP doc itself flags as R8's known risk (its own examples are drawn from ch3 and
ch4, not ch8). That specific hard failure mode simply isn't present at volume
here — worth stating plainly since it's the shape the proposal pre-emptively
worried about most.
