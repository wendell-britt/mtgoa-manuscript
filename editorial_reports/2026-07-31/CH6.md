**Summary.** Chapter 6 is structurally sound in its apparatus and weak in its evidence.
The five `### Move N:` headings are present and taught to a consistent five-part template
(what it is / why it matters / in practice / example / the test). The stage sequence is
five beats — Observe, Model, Design, Deploy, Hand Off — and it is genuinely load-bearing
later (ch6:267, 269, 321, 343, 537), which is more than most chapters manage. The EA
channel table is correct against ch3 on all five rows, including the satisfaction states
and the *I can —* restores. The daemon's position in the Forest line checks out against
ch2. `Appendix F: The Polarity Map` resolves to a real file. No gate walk, no Reflection
Prompts, no banned words. What the chapter does not have is a person. Its answer to
"what does design look like when it changes a condition" is delivered five times, in
Section 6, in five examples containing no human being and no allyship — a dashboard, a
meeting cadence, an onboarding flow, three tools, a feature. The four warmest passages in
the chapter are all in the margin, in somebody else's voice.

- **Location:** ch6:400, ch6:416, ch6:432, ch6:458, ch6:474 (body)
- **Evidence:** ch6:400 — "**Example:** A team that's constantly in meetings. The leverage point is almost never the meeting structure. It's usually the decision rights." · ch6:458 — "**Example:** A new dashboard. The minimum version shows one number."
- **Reader problem:** Section 6 opens by promising the real-situation payoff — ch6:380, "Now — what does it actually look like in a real situation?" — and then hands Jordan five product-management vignettes. Not one of the five examples contains a named person, a harm, or an ally. Jordan came to this chapter saying *I've tried planning, it doesn't work*, and the section built to answer her shows five designs whose stakes are onboarding completion and meeting load. - **Category:** structure

## THE COLDNESS QUESTION — where is this chapter most confident, and does the confidence hold?

**Verdict: the confidence holds as argument and fails as evidence, and the failure is
located exactly where the characterisation note predicts — the chapter arrives at the
diagram before the feeling has finished, and its four warmest passages are all in the
margin, in a different character's voice.**

The chapter's most confident stretch is ch6:153–167 and ch6:223–237: the system-design
axiom ("Every system produces the outcomes it's designed to produce," ch6:155; "Call this
an engineering fact rather than a metaphor," ch6:157) and the Hand Off apologia
(ch6:235–237). Both are excellently argued. Both are argued rather than shown. The axiom
is quietly conceded by the chapter's own polarity twenty lines later ([C4]); the Hand Off
case rests on a rate nobody measured ([C3]). This is the diagnosis the project doctrine
names: the passages where nothing is felt are the passages where the prose is surest.

The distribution of human presence is the measurable form of the problem. The chapter's
living material is at ch6:212 (the council that put its rest hour in the only working
hour of the day — "Not one person in that building was uncaring … I liked them
enormously"), ch6:303–311 (the ledger the annotator will not open and will not throw
out), ch6:441–446 (the withdrawn draft, kept and better than the replacement), and
ch6:18–24. Every one is marginalia. In the body, two passages reach the same temperature:
the Collapse at ch6:331–337, and the single beat at ch6:347–349 — "Nobody has addressed
what was said. The person it was said about is still sitting there." Those are the two
best paragraphs in the chapter and they are surrounded by a hundred lines of system prose
on either side.

Then Section 6, the section that promises "what does it actually look like in a real
situation" (ch6:380), delivers five examples with no people in them at all ([S1]) —
a dashboard, decision rights, an onboarding flow, three tools, a feature — in a book
about allyship. The reader problem is not that the systems language is cold in itself.
Section 3 handles that objection well and honestly: ch6:107, "Anyone who has watched that
happen holds the words at arm's length, rightly. You earned the suspicion, and I am not
going to argue you out of it." The problem is that the chapter earns the trust in Section
3, spends it on abstraction, and then never converts it into a single case where a
designed change reached a person. Jordan is not being lectured at. She is being shown a
system so well that there is nothing left to do.

One further symptom, worth its own line: the body drifts into addressing a senior systems
consultant rather than Jordan. ch6:296 — "The reason you can walk into an organization
and know, before you have seen a single number, that something has gone wrong under the
third floor of the org chart." Jordan's arena is named once, correctly, at ch6:279 —
"a team, a committee, a coalition." Everywhere else the implied reader has vendors
(ch6:520), slides (ch6:347), dashboards (ch6:458), and org charts.
