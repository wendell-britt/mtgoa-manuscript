## SHAMAN · the Body — *What to Do With What You Feel*

**Read the field before the sentence. What happens in Jordan's body when trust arrives?**

Relief, and then a drop. Relief because *benevolence is the one you cannot train* lifts a
weight she has been carrying without a name for it. The drop comes about four seconds
later, when the implication lands: **she has been spending her life on the dimension that
does not move.** That is a grief response, not an insight response, and the current
material has nowhere for her to put it.

**On Q8 — is benevolence ever legitimate.** The Shaman is the only Face that will say
this plainly: **yes, and treating it as a trap will hurt people.** Benevolence is what
the wish to help *feels like from the inside*. If the book tells Jordan the feeling is
worthless, she will read it as *your care was always vanity*, which is the sentence she is
already most afraid of. The framework's honest form is that benevolence is real, it is
just **not a lever**. Feel it; do not aim at it.

**On Q4 — where shadow trust lands.** Chapter 2, without argument. Shadow trust is a
body experience before it is a model. She has to *feel* the Protector arrive before she
can be told it has perfect integrity, and ch2 is where the daemons are met.

**Shaman's ruling:** Q8 — a result, never a target, and say the feeling is not the
problem. Q4 — ch2. And whatever lands in ch1 needs somewhere to put the grief.

## CHALLENGER · the Line — *Who Pays When Nobody Says No*

**Somebody has to say the unwelcome thing, so: this framework is dangerous and the spec
undersells it.**

The spec says trust must never become a score you can farm, and then hands the reader a
three-part model of how trust is constructed. **That is a farming manual.** Anyone
holding it can now work out that capability signals and integrity signals are cheaper
than capability and integrity. `ch1:170`'s worst reader will use this to get better at
seeming reliable.

**Who pays?** The person who then trusts them. Not Jordan — she is not the risk. **The
risk is the reader who is already good at optics and is about to be handed better
optics.**

**The line I would draw:** the framework does not ship without a stated failure mode
in the same section. Not a caveat at the end. **In it.** The book already has the
sentence for it — *"you can hear it in the prose, the hollow ring of a man collecting
tickets instead of playing"* (`ch1:119`).

**On Q3 — does consent appear.** Yes, and stop flinching. The book has an entire
apparatus about not turning people into projects and has used the word *consent* once, in
a footnote about a ledger. **Allyship without consent is the thing the book is against**,
and it currently cannot say so in the plainest available English.

**On Q7 — Mayer credit.** Yes, in `ON_THE_SHOULDERS_OF`, and the Challenger is
unimpressed by the hesitation. The book credits Wilber for an architecture it uses more
loosely than it will use this one.

**Challenger's ruling:** Q3 — name consent. Q7 — credit it. And the failure mode ships
inside the section or the section does not ship.

## REGENT · the Inheritance — *What You Inherited, and What You Do With It*

**What is already here, and what breaks if it moves?**

Three things are load-bearing and were built before this idea existed.

1. **`ch1:91`, the definition.** *"Nothing in it asks whether you are good."* The
   exploration reads this as capability-plus-integrity with benevolence excised. **If
   that reading is correct, the definition is now the framework's proof text, and it must
   not be touched.** Any rewrite of the definition after this point risks losing the
   thing that makes the framework native rather than imported.
2. **`ch2:263`, the Controller.** *"Maintains your conceptual sense of integrity."* This
   sentence has to survive whatever ch2 edit comes next, and it should be flagged in
   `MANUSCRIPT_FILE_CANON` as protected.
3. **`ch1:119`, the counter marked applause.** The benevolence counter. Also protected.

**On Q1 — where the third currency gets built.** With the other two, in ch1, after the
Ticket System. The Regent's reason is not elegance: **an economy introduced in three
places is an economy Jordan will not carry.** She meets tokens and tickets in one sitting.
The third arrives in the same sitting or it arrives as an appendix.

**On Q5 — does each Face get its benevolence definition.** Yes, and this is the cheapest
high-value item on the list. One line per chapter, in a chapter that already opens on
that Face. **It also retro-fits the six chapters into a single argument**, which is
something the book has wanted since the Architect audit.

**Regent's ruling:** Q1 — ch1, with the others. Q5 — yes, and do it before anything
larger. And three lines get marked protected today.

## ARCHITECT · the Design — *Fixing the Condition Instead of the Person*

**Where does this fail structurally, and what makes the right move the easy move?**

**Q2 is the only question that actually blocks anything, and the spec framed it wrong.**
It asks whether the arcade metaphor survives. Wrong question. The right one: **what is
the smallest object that carries *another person's permission* inside an arcade?**

The Architect's answer is that the arcade has one already and the book has not used it.
**The machine does not have to agree to be played — but the arcade has an owner, a floor,
and other people on it.** `ch1:167` is already there: *"Bring the sharpest skeeball arm
in the world to an arcade whose owner is exhausted and going under, and you will not
make a dime, because nobody there is playing."*

**That sentence is the third currency, written a year early.** It says a skill is worth
nothing where nobody is playing with you. The object is not a machine. **It is whether
anybody will play with you**, and an arcade absolutely has that.

So the frame does not break. It extends, and it extends into the one part of an arcade the
book has been ignoring: **there are other people on the floor.** The Arcade has been
described as a room full of machines and one player. That was always the flaw, and it is
the same flaw the closed-loop economy has.

**On Q6 — is *shadow trust* the term.** No. It is a fourth compound on Shadow, daemon and
Face, and it is abstract at exactly the moment the reader needs concrete. The Architect
would name it from the daemon side, where the noun already exists.

**Architect's ruling:** Q2 — the frame extends. The missing object is *who else is on the
floor*, and `ch1:167` already wrote it. Q6 — find a different term.

## DIPLOMAT · the Table — *Why Being Good at Care Is Only Half of It*

**Who is at this table, and what does it cost each of them to stay?**

**The Diplomat's finding is that Chapter 7 has been running this framework for a year
without being told**, and that the retrofit question (`R-T4`) is backwards. `ch7:638`,
`ch7:652` and `ch7:181` are not three sentences to be connected to a new idea in ch1.
**They are the working version. Chapter 1 is the one that needs to catch up.**

Which changes the sequencing: whatever ch1 says about trust has to be *checked against
ch7*, not the other way round, or the book will introduce a model in chapter one that
contradicts the chapter that actually practises it.

**On Q5, and the Diplomat disagrees with the Regent.** Stating each Face's benevolence
definition is not cheap. **It is a promise that each chapter then has to keep.** If the
Diplomat's definition is *nobody left the table*, chapter 7 has to be willing to be
judged against it — including the places where it lets somebody leave. That is a real
cost and it should be paid deliberately rather than as a one-line addition.

**On Q8.** The Diplomat sides with the Shaman and adds the mechanism: **benevolence is
what the other person is actually deciding.** Capability and integrity are what you bring;
benevolence is the verdict they render. Calling it a trap is like calling the scoreboard a
trap. It is the scoreboard.

**Diplomat's ruling:** ch7 leads, ch1 conforms. Q5 — yes, but priced honestly. Q8 —
benevolence is the other person's verdict, and the book should say so.

## SAGE · the Board — *Seeing the Whole Board Without Leaving the Table*

**Which game is actually being played here?**

Not a naming game and not a placement game. **The question underneath all eight is
whether this book is adding a framework or replacing one.**

The Sage's read: **it is replacing one, and nobody has said so.** The book currently
runs on a two-part engine — the Faces and the daemons — and the trust framework is a
third system that sits *underneath* both. Every open question above is easier once that
is admitted, and every one of them is unanswerable while the pretence holds that this is
an addition.

Evidence that it is a replacement rather than an addition: it re-explains the counter
marked applause, the two token kinds, the definition at `ch1:91`, the pre/trans screen,
the daemon template, and the six distortions. **A framework that re-explains six existing
things is not a new room. It is a foundation somebody poured after the house was up.**

**The Sage's warning, and it is the whole job:** a foundation poured late is the most
dangerous kind of good idea, because it makes everything look explicable and tempts a
whole-book rewrite eight weeks from production. **The book does not need the framework
everywhere it applies. It needs it in the two places Jordan gets stuck.**

**On Q4 and Q1 together.** The Sage would put the *minimum* in ch1 and the *depth* in
ch2, and would write down, before either, the list of places the framework is
deliberately **not** going. That list is the deliverable that keeps this from eating
August.

**Sage's ruling:** admit it is a foundation, then use it in two places and write the
do-not-touch list first.
