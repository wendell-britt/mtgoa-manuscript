# Exploration — the trust framework and the ten myths

**2026-08-02. Wendell:**

> *"I have a trust framework that hasn't made it into the book. And we can move our
> allyship myths into the trust framework in a way that's pretty neat. Trust is
> Capability [can I do this] — Integrity [will I do this?] — Benevolence [am I a good
> person]. And the benevolence game is the one that makes people bad allies. Capability
> and integrity can be trained, but because benevolence is perceived, people get into
> battles about perceived benevolence rather than doing the work that actually
> increases impact."*

Exploration only. No prose, no build, no rulings taken.

## 1 · The mapping

The ten myths, `ch1:72`–`81`:

| # | Myth | Dimension | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Allyship means **being good** | **Benevolence** | the pure case. `ch1:205` already calls the other nine *"versions of allyship means being good"* |
| 2 | means **helping the less powerful** | **Benevolence** | with a status premise baked in — it defines the ally as the one with more |
| 3 | means **fixing the problem** | **Capability** | capability applied without asking whether the fix was wanted |
| 4 | means **sacrificing yourself** | **Benevolence** | priced in cost. Depletion as the proof |
| 5 | means **paying down what you owe** | **Benevolence** | retroactive. Proving you are not bad |
| 6 | means **being seen doing it** | **Benevolence** | explicitly the *perceived* kind. This is the counter marked applause |
| 7 | means **saying the right words** | **counterfeit Integrity** | wears integrity's clothes (I follow the rules) and functions as benevolence display |
| 8 | means **following the right people** | **Benevolence** | by association. Borrowed standing |
| 9 | means **never causing harm** | **Benevolence** | defensive. Zero-harm as character evidence |
| 10 | means **having the right framework** | **Capability** | mistaken for it. Knowing the model is not being able to do the thing |

**Benevolence 7. Capability 2. Integrity 0.**

### The result is sharper than a tidy mapping

**Not one of the ten myths is about integrity.** Nothing in the list asks *will I
actually do what I said I would do.* Myth 7 is the closest and it is a counterfeit —
saying the right words is a rule-compliance signal, not a kept promise.

That is not a random distribution of bad beliefs. **It is a systematic avoidance of the
one dimension that is both trainable and directly observable by the other person.**

## 2 · Why benevolence is the trap — the mechanism

Wendell's version: capability and integrity can be trained, benevolence is perceived.
There is a second, harder edge underneath it.

| | Can you train it? | Can it be tested? |
|---|---|---|
| **Capability** | yes | **yes** — you either did the thing or you did not |
| **Integrity** | yes | **yes** — you either did what you said or you did not |
| **Benevolence** | **no** | **no** — it is an attribution, and it lives in the other person's head |

**Benevolence is the only one of the three on which you can never be proven wrong.**

So it is the dimension people choose to fight on. A capability claim gets settled by an
outcome and an integrity claim gets settled by a record; both can end. A benevolence
claim never resolves, in either direction, which means the argument about it is
infinite — and infinite arguments are where people put their effort when they are
frightened of a verdict.

### This is why the game is exhausting, and the book says so without knowing why

`ch1:70`:

> *"instead of asking whether you are good, you start trying to prove it, one act of
> helping at a time"*

**Proving an unfalsifiable claim is unbounded work.** The book already asserts the game
is unwinnable. The trust framework supplies the reason: you cannot win an argument
about an attribution you do not hold. That is not a moral failing or a lack of
discipline. It is the arithmetic of the dimension.

## 3 · The book's own definition is already this move, unnamed

`ch1:91`:

> **"Allyship is the practice of increasing another person's well-being while
> protecting the conditions that allow both of you to remain full players in the
> game."**
>
> *"Nothing in it asks whether you are good."*

Read it against the three dimensions:

- **increasing another person's well-being** — a capability claim. Testable by outcome.
- **protecting the conditions that allow both of you to remain full players** — an
  integrity claim. Testable by whether you held to it.
- **nothing about whether you are good** — benevolence, deliberately excised.

**The definition is capability plus integrity, with benevolence surgically removed, and
the book has never said so.** That sentence — *nothing in it asks whether you are
good* — currently reads as a reassurance to a guilty reader. It is actually a
structural claim about which dimensions can bear weight.

## 4 · What else this explains

**The counter marked applause is the benevolence counter.** `ch1:119` describes what it
pays — *"being seen as good, in the right people nodding, in the post that does
numbers"* — which is a list of benevolence attributions. It now has a name and a reason
rather than being a good image.

**Non-renewable tokens are benevolence fuel.** `ch1:103` lists them: *"guilt,
obligation, the need to be seen as good, the inherited debt you keep trying to
retire."* Every one is an attempt to move a benevolence score. **Renewable tokens come
from the two dimensions you actually own.** That is why one refuels and the other does
not — you can bank a capability, and you cannot bank an opinion somebody else holds.

**It explains the pre/trans screen.** Capability and integrity take time to read;
benevolence signals are instant. So a room under time pressure reads benevolence
signals as a proxy for the other two. Somebody who has gone past the rules stops
emitting those signals — and gets sorted with people who never had the capability at
all. **The screen is not stupid. It is fast, and speed only has benevolence available.**

**It gives the third currency its structure.** `SPEC_TRUST_THE_THIRD_CURRENCY` says
trust is held by the other person and cannot be farmed. The three dimensions say
*which parts* of it you can move: two, honestly, by working; the third, never, and
attempting it is the defect. That is a much more usable rule than *be trustworthy*.

**And it resolves the trap in that spec.** I flagged the risk that trust-as-currency
becomes a score to farm. The three-way split is the safeguard: **you can work on
capability and integrity all you want and it is not manipulation, because both are
real. Working on benevolence directly is the manipulation.** The line is clean and it
falls exactly where the book already wants it.

## 6 · Provenance, which needs a decision

Ability / Benevolence / Integrity is the standard model of trustworthiness from
**Mayer, Davis and Schoorman (1995), *An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust***.
Wendell's *Capability* is their *Ability*; the other two are verbatim.

**No mention of Mayer, Schoorman or the ABI model exists anywhere in the repository.**
The book carries a citation audit and an `ON_THE_SHOULDERS_OF` appendix, and this would
be the second borrowed architecture after Wilber's. It is a decision, not a defect —
but it is one the book's own standards will raise, and it is cheaper to make now than
after the prose is written.

## 7 · Open questions

1. **Does the myth list get reordered by dimension?** Seven benevolence myths in a row
   is a stronger read than the current shuffle, and it makes the count visible without
   stating it.
2. **Does the missing dimension get named?** *There is no myth on this list about
   keeping your word* is a sentence with real force, and it is free.
3. **Where does the framework land** — with the myths at `ch1:64`, with the definition
   at `ch1:91`, or in the economy section as the structure of the third currency?
4. **Does the Myths Read diagnostic score by dimension?** It already sorts the ten;
   sorting them into three groups is a product decision with a book consequence.
5. **Is benevolence ever legitimate?** The framework says you cannot work on it. It does
   not say it is worthless — trust genuinely requires it. The honest version is that it
   is a *result*, not a *target*, and the book should say which.
