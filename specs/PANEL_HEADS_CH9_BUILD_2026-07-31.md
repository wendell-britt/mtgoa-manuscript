## Maera Voss · School of the Body

*from the casebook*

Forty-first entry. Reading the question, a pull behind the sternum, the one that
arrives when somebody asks me to design something and I have not yet been in the
place they are designing for.

Second entry, same day. The error is in the word *build*. A student who is asked
to build reaches for paper. A student who is asked what her hands already do
reaches for the last time they did it.

She has six lines on a sheet. Each one was written in a different week, about a
different failure, and she wrote them while the thing was still warm. That is a
record, and a record is the only honest input a design has.

What I would put in front of her: the six lines, together, for the first time.
Nothing else on the page. **The instruction is to read them as one document and
say what they have in common.** She will find it. They were all written by the
same person about the same fear, and she has never seen them side by side.

Recorded error of mine: I ran this exercise for years asking students what they
wanted to build. The answers were aspirations and they were useless. I stopped
asking, and started handing back their own logs.

What I am working on now is the gap between reading a record accurately and being
able to bear what it says.

## Corin Ash · School of the Line

*from the drill manual*

Cut the question in half. She is not building a game. She is running one, once,
this week, on the person she named in Chapter 1.

You will object that a single rep is not a game. It is. A game is a rule you
agreed to before you knew the outcome, and one rep with a rule and a cost is more
game than a design document nobody plays.

The specification, and do not add to it: **one quest, one person, one Face she
does not reach for, by a date, at a cost she names in advance.** She has thirty
moves. She needs one. She has six Faces. She needs the one the moment requires,
which is the whole book, and she has been told this since Chapter 1.

The date is the part students remove first. Remove the date and it is a plan.
Plans do not fail, which is why people prefer them.

What is still difficult, and it is mine rather than hers: a line drawn on a
schedule lands on somebody who did not choose the schedule. I set dates. I have
not solved what the date costs the other person.

## Sera Quill · School of the Oath

*from the annotated charter*

Clause one. The instrument shall be a written undertaking, not a design. *Note:
a design commits the designer to nothing, and this house has four generations of
designs in the archive and no record of what any of them cost.*

Clause two. It shall name the inheritor. One person, by name, who receives the
practice when the applicant stops. *Note: the applicant will resist this clause
and the resistance is the finding. A practice with no named inheritor was never
being kept for anybody; it was being kept for the keeper.*

Clause three. It shall be dated, and revisited on a date set at the time of
writing rather than when the applicant next feels moved to. *Note: as held under
Merrow, and Merrow was wrong about a great deal, and not about this.*

Clause four. The applicant's six recorded lines shall be entered as the terms of
the undertaking, unedited. *Note: an applicant who improves those lines before
entering them has begun the practice by falsifying its baseline.*

What remains open in this office is how an inheritance is handed to somebody who
will change it, without the change being recorded as damage.

## Irix Vale · School of the Pattern

*from the engineering monograph*

Specification 9.1. The deliverable is one page. See figure eleven.

A one-page constraint is not an aesthetic preference. In practice a design that
requires more than one page has not been reduced, and an unreduced design is a
description of a problem wearing the vocabulary of a solution.

The page carries four fields, and the fields are already specified elsewhere in
this book, which is the argument for using them: the quest from Chapter 1; the
Face the quest requires, which is rarely the Face she has; the single move; and
the condition that will cause the design to fail. In practice the fourth field is
the only one that predicts anything.

Tolerance. This holds for one person and one quest. Past that it fails, and it
fails in a specific way — she will begin designing for a category of person, and
a category cannot tell you it is unhappy.

Do not ask me to estimate how many designs a student will abandon before one
holds. The estimate is the failure.

What is being worked on here: asking before specifying. The one-page form was
issued for four years before anybody asked a student whether one page was where
the difficulty was.

## Elian Cross · School of the Bridge

*from the negotiation casebook*

Two positions, and both were put to this office in the same week.

The first: *"I want to build something that helps."* Recorded verbatim. It is a
sincere position and it has no counterparty in it, which is why the office
receives it so often and can do so little with it.

The second, from a delegate who had already tried: *"I built the thing. Nobody
came. I do not know whether that means it was wrong or whether I never asked
anyone."*

Between those two positions is the instrument. It is not a design and it is not a
rep. **It is a conversation with one named person, scheduled, in which she says
what she is making and asks what it would cost them to use it.**

She will want to build first and ask afterward. Every party this office has
received wanted that, and the ones who did it in that order arrived here later
with the second position.

What each party is protecting: she is protecting the design from a verdict before
it is ready. The other person is protecting an evening. Both are legitimate, and
the terms that hold are the ones where she brings something unfinished enough
that the other person can still change it.

The office does not record who was right. It records that eleven years of this
work have not produced a single case where asking first cost more than asking
after.

## Thalen Orr · School of the Horizon

*a commentary on the other five*

Corin would have her run one rep by Friday and Corin is right within the Line,
where a thing that has not been paid for has not been learned; Irix would have
her reduce it to a page and Irix is right within the Pattern, where an unreduced
design is a confession; Sera would have her name an inheritor before she has
built anything worth inheriting, which sounds premature and is the only one of
the five that survives her losing interest; Maera would refuse to let her design
at all until she has read her own six lines as one document, which is the sort of
delay that looks like obstruction and is usually the whole answer; and Elian
would have her ask somebody before she is ready, which is the hardest of the five
and the one every student postpones.

They are describing one instrument from five seats, and the reason we can say
that with confidence is that the book already built it: **the sequence is Review,
Discern, Design, Build, Pass On**, and Maera is Review, Irix is Discern and
Design, Corin is Build, Elian is the test that keeps Build honest, and Sera is
Pass On — which is either a coincidence or evidence that the author knew what he
was doing before he asked us.

What we would add, and it is the only thing in this document that is ours: the
chapter should not ask her what she wants to build. It should show her that she
has been building it since Chapter 1 and hand her the page where it already
exists. **A person who is told to start will start something new. A person who is
shown what she has already accumulated will finish it.**

We are not certain whether that is wisdom or a preference for tidiness. It has
been both before.
