## Appendix C — scope the worked scenarios

Replace `APPENDIX_C_FIVE_CHANNELS.md:56–64` with:

> You're in a difficult conversation. A question about your work lands like a public challenge, and anger is one possible response.
>
> **Wake (2 seconds):** The question stays with you. You notice anger.
> **Open (1 second):** Use WAVE to make room for that response before answering.
> **Clean (3 seconds):** Ask: *What boundary or value might this anger be pointing toward?* One possibility is that being questioned in public matters to you. That does not establish that the other person intended an attack.
> **Grow (3 seconds):** Hold what you know: a question about your work does not settle your competence.
> **Show (2 seconds):** Respond from that clarity: “I hear the question. Here is what happened.”
>
> Total: about eleven seconds. Make enough room to answer without turning your first response into the whole conversation. The practice neither establishes the questioner's intent nor requires the anger to disappear.

Replace `:68–76` with:

> Something difficult happened, and you have time to stay with the sadness it brought up.
>
> **Wake:** Name the loss as specifically as you can.
> **Open:** Use WAVE to let the response be present without forcing it away or turning it into a conclusion.
> **Clean:** Ask: *What mattered enough to grieve here?* An answer may be: a relationship that cannot continue in the form it had.
> **Grow:** Let that answer change the story you are telling yourself: the relationship was real; its ending does not, by itself, prove that you are broken.
> **Show:** Choose one next act that fits the loss: cry, call a friend, rest, or write down what you want to carry forward.
>
> This may take minutes or hours. The practice offers a clearer relationship to what you lost and a next move you can stand behind; it does not require a particular emotional result.

> The **3-2-1 practice** gives you three vantage points: observing a charged figure, talking with it, and speaking as it. It can reveal what your reaction is protecting and give you more than one response to choose from.

## Appendix F — make the Polarity Map observable

Replace `APPENDIX_F_POLARITY_MAP.md:14–20` with:

> You have three tools for a stuck moment, and they are not interchangeable.
>
> - Use **3-2-1** when a person or figure has taken over your account of a situation.
> - Use **WAVE** when a response is already present and you need a brief pause before acting.
> - Use the **Polarity Map** when you are torn between two things that both seem right, and choosing one seems to keep recreating the problem.
>
> A polarity may be present when you solve a problem from one direction and soon have to solve its opposite: more loyalty produces stagnation; more reform produces rupture. A problem may still need a practical solution. The map helps you test whether the tension is asking you to hold two goods rather than declare one of them wrong.

Replace `:32–44` with:

> **1. Name the situation.** Use a specific scene, not an abstract issue: “Yesterday's meeting when I said nothing,” not “speaking up at work.”
>
> **2. Name the two poles.** Both need to be legitimate and actionable within ten minutes. If a pole is only a virtue, name the behavior that would express it.
>
> **3. Mark your position.** Put the dot where you have been operating this week. Which pole have you been over-expressing? Which have you left out? The dot is a diagnostic, not a verdict.
>
> **4. Read the dot.** Look for observable costs. What has happened repeatedly while you have stayed at this end? What has gone missing from the agreement, the relationship, or the work?
>
> **5. Design one integrated action.** Write one move that carries both poles at once. The integrated-sentence template is the fastest way in:
>
> *“I stay with ________ because it still ________, **and** I need ________ to change before I can keep showing up honestly.”*
>
> The word doing the work is *and*. If the sentence names both commitments and a person involved could answer it, you have a move to test.
