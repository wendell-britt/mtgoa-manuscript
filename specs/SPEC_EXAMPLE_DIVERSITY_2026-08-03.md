# SPEC — Example diversity, and the claim the examples contradict

**2026-08-03. Wendell**, on a drafted ch1 opener set in a meeting:

> *"We're overusing the example of someone getting talked over in a meeting. This is
> worth scanning the whole book for because we want to give a diverse set of examples
> of how the work in this book can be used to apply to people."*

Scanned. **The repetition he caught is the small version of it.** *Talked over* runs
twice. `meeting` runs **67 times**, and the setting problem is structural rather than
lexical.

---

## 1 · The finding

**35 `**Example:**` blocks across ch3–ch9. Twenty are unambiguously workplace, and
thirty-one are workplace-adjacent** once committees, working groups, review boards,
all-hands and coalitions are counted.

| setting | Examples |
|---|---|
| workplace | **20** |
| workplace-adjacent (committee, working group, review board, coalition) | 11 |
| online | 2 |
| family or home | 1 |
| **outside work entirely** | **4** |

The four: a letting agent and a family viewing a flat (`ch7:570`), a clinician calling
Nia's mother non-compliant (`ch7:598`), Kit's mother's funeral (`ch8:649`), and Ade
holding a test result (`ch8:683`). **Three of the four are in ch7 and ch8.**

**ch3, ch4 and ch5 are 100% workplace.** Fifteen consecutive Examples without leaving
an office.

### The transfer drills do not rescue it

`ch9:634`–`:644` is the book's transfer test, six numbered scenarios, and it is the one
place a reader is asked to choose a Face for a situation rather than watch one chosen.
Of the six: a team meeting, a volunteer group, **a friend telling you about her job**,
an organization's founding ritual, two coalition organizations, and a meeting you have
argued in for two years. **Five of six are organizational.** The friend at `:638` is the
only scenario that is one person talking to one person about their own life, which is
the shape `authors_note:35` and `ch9:526` both say the whole practice reduces to.

---

## 2 · Why this is a defect rather than a preference

**`ch1:44` makes the opposite claim, in the book's own voice:**

> *"Real allyship happens in plenty of places that game never reaches, and there,
> fluency earns you nothing. Children receive care or not based on whether you actually
> understand what they need… People from cultural traditions different enough from
> yours meet the language of allyship as an import."*

The paragraph names children and cultural traditions as the places the fluency game
cannot reach. **No Example in the book involves a child.** None involves a cultural
tradition met as an import. The chapter asserts that the important work happens outside
the professional-managerial arena and then demonstrates allyship inside it, thirty-one
times.

**That is not a diversity checkbox. It is a claim the evidence contradicts**, and a
reader who is not in an office has been told the book is for her and shown that it is
not.

### It also narrows who the reader can be

Measured on the power the reader holds in each Example:

| the reader is | Examples |
|---|---|
| holding institutional power (you built it, you run it, you took over the calendar, you are on the hiring panel) | 8 |
| a peer inside the same institution | 7 |
| outside the institution, or with less standing than it | 4 |

**The default reader has a team, a budget, a calendar to take over and a panel to sit
on.** She is a manager. That is one reader, and the book's thesis about burnout applies
at least as hard to people with none of those.

### Stakes are narrow in the same direction

Job and career stakes appear in nine Examples; housing and money in five; health,
illness and death in four; belonging and voice in four. **The most common thing at risk
in this book is somebody's job.**

---

## 3 · What is already good, and should not be touched

**The naming is genuinely diverse and it was clearly deliberate.** Seventy-one distinct
proper nouns appear in the Examples, and the people in them are Ines, Ravi, Nadia,
Priya, Tomas, Dara, Yusuf, Ana, Marcus, Meera, Dele, Nia, Alan, Ellis, Sam, Kit, Ade,
Femi, Tess, Rosa, Bea and Ruth.

**So the book did the work on *who* and never did it on *where*.** That is worth saying
plainly, because it means the fix is not a rewrite of the Examples' people. It is a
relocation of some of their scenes.

---

## 4 · The rule this produces

**No more than two consecutive Examples in the same setting, and every chapter's set of
five covers at least three of the five arenas.**

| arena | what it is |
|---|---|
| **workplace** | the current default. Keep it. It is where many readers live |
| **family and household** | the parent whose politics make you wince (`ch1:50` already names this and no Example uses it), the sibling, the dinner table, the group chat that is relatives |
| **friendship** | one person telling another about their own life, which is the shape the whole practice reduces to, and which appears once in nine chapters |
| **community and public** | the clinic, the letting agent, the school, the bus, the queue, the congregation, the mutual aid group |
| **caregiving across a power gap the reader did not create** | children, elders, anyone whose access to the thing runs through you |

**The fifth arena is the one `ch1:44` promised and the book never delivers**, and it is
also where the tower-defense frame is hardest and most honest, because a child's
Protector does not care what vocabulary you have.

---

## 5 · The cost, honestly

**Relocating an Example is not free.** Each one is tuned to its chapter's move and
several depend on institutional machinery to make the move legible — `ch5:571`'s
standing Friday review, `ch6:522`'s escalation path, `ch4:706`'s hiring panel. Those are
Examples where the *system* is the subject, and moving them to a kitchen would break
them.

**So the target is not parity. It is the removal of the run.** Fifteen consecutive
workplace Examples across ch3, ch4 and ch5 is the thing a reader notices. Changing
**four** of those fifteen, one per chapter plus one, clears the run without touching any
Example whose move needs an institution.

### The cheapest four

| | current | move it to |
|---|---|---|
| `ch3:784` | you noticed months ago that Jo is not disorganised | a sibling, a household, the same noticing |
| `ch4:652` | Tomas asks for a start date in writing | a landlord, a school, the same unanswered ask |
| `ch5:591` | the intake form's legal name field, Ana correcting it | a clinic or a school form, which is the same defect with more at stake |
| `ch6:448` | Meera asks three times for a screen-reader format | a public service or a place of worship |

**None of the four needs its move rewritten.** Each one needs its furniture changed.

---

## 6 · Open

1. **Does `ch1:44` get amended, or do the Examples get relocated to match it?** The
   claim is good and the book should keep it, which argues for relocating.
2. **Does any Example involve a child?** `ch1:44` names children first and the book
   never goes there. It is the largest single gap and probably the hardest to write
   without condescension.
3. **Does the reader-as-manager default get named anywhere?** If most Examples assume a
   team and a budget, the book could say so once rather than let a reader without them
   conclude she is not the audience.
4. **`ch9:638` is the model.** *A friend is telling you about something that happened at
   her job, and about ninety seconds in you notice a spike in your chest you have not
   named.* One person, one relationship, no institution, and the move is entirely
   internal. **More of the book should look like that one.**
