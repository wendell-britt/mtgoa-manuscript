**Prediction going in was that Adams would split like Wilber and Snicket — importable
register versus not. He doesn't, and the reason is more useful than the split would have
been: every quotable Adams line is the payoff of an unquotable setup.** The dolphins line
needs the paragraph. "You're a jerk, Dent" needs the silver ship. You cannot import the
payoff without importing the structure that pays it off, which is the whole case against
building a corpus out of quotations, stated a third time.

## Excerpt 1 — the cave, and the alien who came a long way to insult him

The regular early morning yell of horror was the sound of Arthur Dent waking up and suddenly remembering where he was.

It wasn't just that the cave was cold, it wasn't just that it was damp and smelly. It was that the cave was in the middle of Islington and there wasn't a bus due for two million years.

Time is the worst place, so to speak, to get lost in, as Arthur Dent could testify, having been lost in both time and space a good deal. At least being lost in space kept you busy.

He was stranded on prehistoric Earth as the result of a complex sequence of events that had involved his being alternately blown up and insulted in more bizarre regions of the Galaxy than he had ever dreamed existed, and though life had now turned very, very, very quiet, he was still feeling jumpy.

He hadn't been blown up now for five years.

He had hardly seen anyone since he and Ford Prefect had parted company four years previously, and he hadn't been insulted in all that time either.

Except just once.

It had happened on a spring evening about two years ago.

He was returning to his cave just a little after dusk when he became aware of lights flashing eerily through the clouds. He turned and stared, with hope suddenly clambering through his heart. Rescue. Escape. The castaway's impossible dream—a ship.

And as he watched, as he stared in wonder and excitement, a long silver ship descended through the warm evening air, quietly, without fuss, its long legs unlocking in a smooth ballet of technology.

It alighted gently on the ground, and what little hum it had generated died away, as if lulled by the evening calm.

A ramp extended itself.

Light streamed out.

A tall figure appeared silhouetted in the hatchway. It walked down the ramp and stood in front of Arthur.

"You're a jerk, Dent," it said simply.

It was alien, very alien. It had a peculiar alien tallness, a peculiar alien flattened head, peculiar slitty little alien eyes, extravagantly draped golden robes with a peculiarly alien collar design, and pale gray green alien skin that had that lustrous sheen about it that most gray green races can acquire only with plenty of exercise and very expensive soap.

Arthur boggled at it.

It gazed levelly at him.

Arthur's first sensations of hope and trepidation had instantly been overwhelmed by astonishment, and all sorts of thoughts were battling for the use of his vocal cords at this moment.

### Moves

**1. Reversed information order.** *"The regular early morning yell of horror was the sound
of Arthur Dent waking up."* Normal order names the actor, then the sound. This names the
sound as an established routine, then reveals its cause. **"Regular" is the entire joke** —
it converts horror into a habit. Eight words in.

**2. Ladder negation.** *"It wasn't just that the cave was cold, it wasn't just that it was
damp and smelly. It was that..."* — see `RULE_COLLISIONS.md`, this breaks B.1 as currently
written and forces a third scope.

**3. Category error stated flat.** *"Time is the worst place, so to speak, to get lost in."*
The "so to speak" is the deadpan acknowledgment that he knows time isn't a place. He does it
anyway. This is the philosophical joke in its minimum viable form.

**4. Beat paragraphs.** *"He hadn't been blown up now for five years."* — one sentence, its
own paragraph. **"Now" is doing everything**: it implies being blown up is an ongoing
condition currently in remission. Then *"Except just once."* — two words, its own paragraph,
and it functions as a pivot that reopens the story. Purest available exemplar of
fragment-as-beat in landing position.

**5. Ceremony built in short declaratives.** *"A ramp extended itself. / Light streamed
out."* Separate one-line paragraphs. The white space is doing the pacing; the sentences are
plain on purpose so the reader supplies the awe.

**6. Bathos as register drop, not just scale drop.** Silver ship, ballet of technology, the
castaway's impossible dream — then *"You're a jerk, Dent," it said simply.* The deflation
isn't merely smaller, it's from high romantic register into playground insult, and the
attribution *"said simply"* is the flattest possible verb after the biggest possible buildup.

**7. THE CONCEIT, at sentence scale.** *"peculiar alien tallness... peculiar alien flattened
head... peculiar slitty little alien eyes... peculiarly alien collar design... alien skin"* —
the word run five times in one sentence, closing on **"very expensive soap,"** which drops
from cosmic-exotic to domestic-mundane.

This matters: **the flagship register is not a Snicket quirk. It is a family, and Adams runs
it at sentence scale rather than paragraph scale.** Sentence scale is far more usable — one
sentence with a word repeated five times is placeable anywhere, where a full "stop"-style
paragraph needs a whole slot. This is the version to reach for first.

**8. Paired contrast beats.** *"Arthur boggled at it. / It gazed levelly at him."* Two short
sentences, parallel structure, opposed verbs. "Boggled" versus "gazed levelly" carries the
entire power differential.

## Excerpt 2 — perfectly normal paranoia

"You know," said Arthur thoughtfully, "all this explains a lot of things. All through my life I've had this strange unaccountable feeling that something was going on in the world, something big, even sinister, and no one would tell me what it was." "No," said the old man, "that's just perfectly normal paranoia. Everyone in the Universe has that."

### The move: category reassignment

Arthur offers something that sounds like hard-won personal insight. The old man reclassifies
it as a universal, standard-issue condition. The joke is the **downgrade from significance to
statistics** — not "you're wrong," but "you're ordinary."

**Why this one matters more than it looks.** This is the move for handling a reader's
self-important suffering without scolding her. *That isn't your special damage, that's the
standard kit.* It relieves and deflates in the same breath, and it never once says she is
wrong to have felt it.

For a book whose reader arrives guilt-driven and convinced her guilt is both uniquely hers
and evidence of her seriousness, this may be the single most valuable import in the corpus.
It is Adams doing, in two lines of dialogue, what Snicket's unreconciled attribution does in
one sentence — and it is gentler, because the reassignment is company rather than correction.

## Excerpt 3 — dolphins and mice

It is an important and popular fact that things are not always what they seem. For instance, on the planet Earth, man had always assumed that he was more intelligent than dolphins because he had achieved so much—the wheel, New York, wars and so on—while all the dolphins had ever done was muck about in the water having a good time. But conversely, the dolphins had always believed that they were far more intelligent than man—for precisely the same reasons. Curiously enough, the dolphins had long known of the impending destruction of the planet Earth and had made many attempts to alert mankind to the danger; but most of their communications were misinterpreted as amusing attempts to punch footballs or whistle for tidbits, so they eventually gave up and left the Earth by their own means shortly before the Vogons arrived.

"Look," said Arthur, "would it save you a lot of time if I just gave up and went mad now?" For a while the aircar flew on in awkward silence. Then the old man tried patiently to explain. "Earthman, the planet you lived on was commissioned, paid for, and run by mice. It was destroyed five minutes before the completion of the purpose for which it was built, and we've got to build another one." Only one word was registering with Arthur. "Mice?" he said. "Indeed, Earthman." "Look, sorry, are we talking about the little white furry things with the cheese fixation and women standing on tables screaming in early sixties sitcoms?"

### Moves

**1. Mock-authoritative opener.** *"It is an important and popular fact."* Facts are not
popular. The inflated framing is the joke, and it sets a register the paragraph then keeps
with total composure.

**2. The symmetry joke — and this one is structurally yours.** Two parties reach opposite
conclusions about their own superiority **"for precisely the same reasons."** Both sides,
identical logic, opposite verdicts, stated without taking a side.

That is a book about allyship across difference, compressed into one clause. It is also the
only humor structure in the corpus that generates real charge without a target — nobody is
being mocked, the *shape* is. Given the standing ban on jokes that are at the reader rather
than beside her, this is the safest high-yield joke form available.

**3. The list that collapses moral scale.** *"the wheel, New York, wars and so on."*
Technology, civilization, atrocity, shrug. Four items, descending, ending in dismissal.

**4. Comic focus after a cosmic reveal.** The planet was run by mice and destroyed five
minutes early — and *"only one word was registering with Arthur. 'Mice?'"* The character
latches onto the smallest detail. This is how you keep a reader inside an overwhelming
disclosure: let someone in the text fail to take it all in.

**5. Pop-culture reference — RULE VALIDATED.** *"the little white furry things with the
cheese fixation and women standing on tables screaming in early sixties sitcoms."* The
reference is to sixties sitcoms, which most current readers have not seen. **It works
anyway**, because the image is fully self-contained — you get the picture without the
referent, and the referent is a second layer if you have it.

This is the standing pop-culture rule (*the sentence must work fully for someone who doesn't
get it*) confirmed by evidence rather than asserted. Promote it out of hypotheses.
