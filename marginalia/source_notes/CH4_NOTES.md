## NOTE 2 — **ARC BEAT** — *confess / evade*
**Placement:** Section 3, immediately after *"Very few people can do all three without
flinching."*

*Moved from Section 1. The original floated — it said "one of these" while the adjacent prose
was still telling the village myth, so there was no "these" to point at. See the standing rule
below.*

> *Very few people can do all three without flinching. Somebody did all three to me once, in
> eleven words, and then went back to what they had been doing.*
>
> *There was nothing to say back. That is the part nobody mentions — it was not cruel and it
> left me no opening, and those two together meant I had no move at all. I stood there a moment
> and then I left. I have taught this to four hundred students since. I have used somebody
> else's example every single time.*

*Third pass. The second draft ended on *"what I recognised at the time was the floor"* — a
figure the reader had to unpack, which steals the beat the punchline needs. Replaced with the
plain report: a clean no delivered well **removes every response you have.** No opening to
argue with, no unkindness to be wounded by. That is more useful to her than a wince, and it is
what actually happened.*

*Two repairs from the first draft. **"Eleven words" is now load-bearing** — the prose has just said almost nobody can
do all three, so eleven words is the virtuoso version and the reader will count. And the evasion
became a **pattern** rather than a single refusal: four hundred times, somebody else's example,
every occasion. A cost paid repeatedly reads better on the second pass, and on the first pass it
is still only dry.*

## STANDING RULE — every note grabs a phrase

**A note must take hold of something specific in the prose beside it**, in its first sentence.
Corin's interrupting, the Force ↔ Restraint polarity, the Auditor, *all three without
flinching.*

A note that opens on its own subject is a personal anecdote parked next to a chapter. It will
read as vague however good the sentences are, because the reader has nothing to attach it to —
and the failure is invisible while drafting, since the note reads fine on its own.

**Check while writing:** what phrase in the adjacent paragraph is this note holding? If the
answer takes more than a second, move the note or change its first line.

---

## STANDING RULE — clear, not clever

**No figure the reader has to unpack.** Adams and Snicket are immediately legible; the surprise
lives in what is reported, never in whether the sentence can be parsed.

A metaphor that costs a beat of decoding spends the beat the punchline needed, and the reader
arrives at the joke slightly behind. This is the failure mode of writing *well* rather than
writing *clearly*, and it is hardest to catch in your own best sentences — the more pleased you
are with a figure, the more likely it is doing this.

**Check:** could a tired reader take this at speed, once? If it needs a second pass, say the
plain thing. The plain thing is usually more specific anyway, which is where the interest
actually was.

## NOTE 3 — *warn*
**Placement:** Section 3, at the Polarity Encounter — Force ↔ Restraint.

> *Corin's blind spot lives in this section, and they know it, which helps less than you would
> hope.*
>
> *They cannot reliably tell slow from frightened. Nor can you yet. A person taking their time
> is gathering something; a person who is frightened is waiting for the situation to change on
> its own. So the test is not how long they take — it is whether anything is arriving. Ask what
> they are waiting for. If they can tell you, it is not cowardice. If they say "just a bit
> longer," you have your answer, and so do they.*

*Names a Head's canonical blind spot and then supplies the thing that fixes it — which is what
keeps the margin from being commentary.*

## NOTE 4 — *amend* — **the chapter's only one**
**Placement:** Section 4, at "Run the 30-Second Protocol in the Village."

> *Thirty seconds is generous. You will have four.*
>
> *In four seconds you can do one thing, so make it the same thing every time: say what you
> would say if you were not going to be in the room afterwards. Do not work out which line to
> draw. Draw the one you already know about. The protocol is for afterwards, when you are
