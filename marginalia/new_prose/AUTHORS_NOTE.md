# A Note Before the School
### *author's note — draft 1, 2026-07-28*

**Placement:** front matter, before Chapter 1.
**Voice:** Wendell, undisguised. This is the only place in the book he is not in a mask, and
that contrast is load-bearing — it is what makes the unsigned marginalia read as somebody
else, which is what the Chapter 8 reveal runs on.
**Title:** deliberately uses the existing *A Note Before the ___* device. The reader learns the
convention from the author on page one, then meets it again inside the treatises.
**Length:** ~470 words.

---

I finished this book twice.

The first version was correct. Six years of work, every claim sourced, every practice run on
somebody who was not me. I read it end to end and felt nothing at all. There is a particular
disappointment in producing something accurate and dead, and I sat in that for a while, and
then I did the thing I have always done, which is add more rigor.

That did not help. Rigor was not what was missing.

What was missing was that I was not enjoying myself, and neither would you.

So I stopped writing a book about allyship and started building a school.

The school does not exist. Its six Heads do not exist. Irix Vale, who wrote the fourth treatise
and who cannot stop being enchanted by his own diagrams, is a person I made up in order to say
something true about a thing I have watched capable people do for a very long time.

The school is on a ship. The ship is called the Calrunia and it has been lost for a long while,
and the staff responded to that by starting a school, which is either the most sensible or the
least sensible response to being lost that anyone has ever had, and I have not settled on
which. It puts in at worlds. One of the worlds is yours. That is why these documents keep
describing your workplace as though it were an excavation site — because from where the Heads
sit, it is one, and I could not find a faster way to make your Tuesday visible to you than to
have it described by somebody who has never had one.

The students come from those worlds. Not the ones who won. The ones who stayed — who did the
work at home, badly, for years, mostly without being thanked, and got something out of the
staying that could not be got any other way. When the ship puts in, a few of them decide they
would rather take what they learned somewhere it is needed than remain where it has finally
become comfortable. It costs them their home. They go anyway. I have never met one who
described that as a sacrifice, which is how you can tell that it was.

You will also find annotations in the margins. They are not mine. You will find out in the
eighth chapter whose they are, and I would rather you found out then.

Here is the rule I followed about making things up. **The world is invented. The problem is
not.** Every structure the Heads describe is one I have stood inside, and a number of them I
helped build. The wellness hour is real. The role description written by somebody who has
never done the role is real. When a treatise tells you what happens in a community that keeps
producing the outcome it says it does not want, that is reportage in a costume. I gave myself
permission to invent the school. I did not give myself permission to invent the findings.

And then the last thing, which is the part I would ask you to actually keep.

I have spent six years arguing that play is not the opposite of serious work — that play is how
a person metabolizes what earnestness can only describe. I believed it. I taught it. I could
not finish a book about it, because I was writing the book earnestly, and earnest is precisely
the state in which nothing moves.

The book got finished the week I started playing with it.

I would like to tell you I planned that as a demonstration. I did not. I walked into the
strongest evidence I have for the whole argument with the manuscript already at the printer,
and the evidence is that you are holding this at all.

## Notes for revision

**Verify before print — I do not have these and did not want to invent them:**
- *"a very long time"* — swap in the real span if you want the specific.
- *"the week I started playing with it"* — if the real unit is days, say days. The specific is
  better than the round number.
- The closing beat currently says *with the manuscript already at the printer.* True this week,
  and it may read as too-clever in year two. Alternative: *four days before it went to print* —
  more concrete, ages the same way. Or cut the timing entirely and end on *the evidence is that
  you are holding this at all.*

**Structural choices, for overruling:**
1. **The frame is explained in one paragraph and never again.** No ship history, no founding,
   no cosmology. Rick does not explain the Citadel.
2. **Irix Vale is named and the other five are not.** One named Head is enough to establish
   that they are people; naming all six in front matter turns the note into a cast list.
3. **The Ch8 reveal is announced but not described.** *You will find out in the eighth chapter*
   creates the expectation without setting anything up, which is what keeps the reveal cheap.
4. **Calrunia is the ship, not the setting.** The world it came from is never named or
   described, so **Earth remains the only named place in the book** — which keeps the reader's
   own world as the exotic one. The ecosystem link is present; the canon debt is not.

5. **Recruitment standard: stayed, not won.** Deliberate, and worth defending. If the bar is
   *saved their own world*, the reader cannot board — she has not saved anything, she is tired,
   and she suspects her own caring is performance. The criterion is having been in it. That is
   a berth she can take, and it also reframes allyship away from penance: these are not people
   working off guilt, they are people moving competence they already earned.

6. **The reader is a recruit, which makes the back matter native.** She is from a world the
   ship put in at. Enrollment is not a pitch appended to a book — it is the thing the book has
   been describing. No sales register required anywhere.

7. **The lost-ship premise is one sentence and never explained.** No dimensional mechanics, no
   how, no whether they get back. The premise earns its place because it *is* the thesis —
   arriving somewhere that did not send for you, helping with tools nobody asked for, leaving
   without learning whether it worked — and because it gives the Headmaster's grief a cause the
   RPG had listed without one. Foreground stays the school. The loss stays background; Voyager
   works when it is about the crew's Tuesday, not about being lost.
8. **No apology anywhere.** The note explains a choice; it does not ask permission for it. The
   first draft of any author's note like this wants to say *I hope you'll bear with me.* It has
   been kept out.
