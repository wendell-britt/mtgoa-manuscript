## What a Polarity Is

A polarity is two necessary capacities that become costly when separated.

Each side has a gift when it's integrated and a cost when it's isolated. Holding boundaries keeps you from disappearing. Creating belonging keeps you from becoming a fortress. Neither side is the villain. The villain is the belief that you have to choose one and exile the other permanently.

**The map is simple:**

```
    POLE A  ←——————————●——————————→  POLE B
                  (you are here)
```

Draw a line. Name the poles. Mark where you are today — closer to A, closer to B, or stuck in the middle pretending you haven't chosen.

The dot is not a verdict. It is a diagnostic. You are allowed to move.

---

## How to Draw Your Map (Full Method)

**1. Name the charge.** What moment are you stuck in? Not the abstract issue — the specific scene. The meeting. The text thread. The coalition call where you went quiet and haven't stopped replaying it.

**2. Name the two poles.** Both must be things you could actually *do* in the next ten minutes — behaviors, not identities. Not "confident vs insecure." Try "making a direct ask" ↔ "reading the room before speaking." If one side sounds purely bad, you haven't found the polarity yet — you've found a judgment.

**3. Mark your position.** Where are you on the axis right now? Which pole are you over-expressing? Which are you suppressing to keep the peace?

**4. Design one integrated action.** Not A instead of B. Not B instead of A. One move that contains both: *"I'm not fully ready to commit to this timeline, and I want to stay in the conversation until we find one that works."* If the action only has one pole in it, keep designing.

## Three Allyship Polarities (Worked Examples)

**Holding boundaries ↔ Creating belonging**

*The stuck moment:* You need to say no to a request from someone you care about in the movement. Saying no feels like abandonment. Saying yes feels like self-erasure.

*The map:* Left pole — naming limits, protecting capacity, refusing to perform availability you don't have. Right pole — staying in relationship, signaling care, making room for the other person's disappointment without leaving the field.

*Integrated move:* "I can't take this on. I want you to know that's about my capacity, not my commitment to you — and I'm here to talk about what would actually help."

---

**Speaking truth ↔ Maintaining safety**

*The stuck moment:* Something needs to be said in the room. If you say it, the field might rupture. If you don't, the silence becomes complicity.

*The map:* Left pole — naming what is actually happening, refusing to perform agreement, letting the charge land. Right pole — holding enough safety that the truth can be heard without the conversation becoming a demolition.

*Integrated move:* "I want to name something I haven't said yet, and I want to say it in a way that keeps us in the room together. Can I try?"

---

**Care ↔ Impact**

*The stuck moment:* You are showing up with warmth, checking in, making space — and nothing is moving. Or you are pushing for outcomes — and the relationships are thinning out.

*The map:* Left pole — care: attunement, harm reduction, presence that doesn't cost the other person their dignity. Right pole — impact: material change, decisions that stick, presence that costs you something real.

*Integrated move:* "I care about how this lands for you, and I also need us to decide something today. Both are true. Let's do both."

## Polarities Are Not Problems

When you treat a polarity like a problem, you keep solving the same side over and over. You solve for belonging by abandoning boundaries. You solve for safety by swallowing truth. You solve for care by avoiding the hard decision. And then the suppressed pole comes back as resentment, rupture, or burnout — because it was never optional. It was waiting.

Polarity management is slower than problem-solving and more honest. You stop asking *which side is right* and start asking *what does this moment need from both sides.*

## Generic Practice — Your Polarity Map

Pick one stuck moment from this week. Work the four steps on paper:

1. **Charge:** One sentence. The scene.
2. **Poles:** Two behaviors. Both legitimate. Both actionable.
3. **Dot:** Where are you on the axis?
4. **Integrated move:** One sentence you could actually say or do that holds both.

Sit with the integrated move for thirty seconds. Notice whether it feels slightly uncomfortable and clear — not righteous, not clever. That feeling is often the signal that you found a real polarity instead of a fake problem.

## Connection to 3-2-1 and the WAVE-Spiral

These three tools answer different questions:

- **3-2-1:** *Who owns this charge?* (Projection → reintegration)
- **Polarity Map:** *What structural tension am I inside?* (Two rights → integrated action)
- **WAVE-Spiral:** *How do I move the charge once I know what it is?* (Wake → Clean → Grow → Show)

Use 3-2-1 when the trigger is a *person* or *part*. Use the Polarity Map when the trigger is *I can't choose between two things that both seem necessary*. Use WAVE when the charge is already in your body and ready to move.

Many hard allyship moments need all three — in that order, or circling back until the loop completes.
