# The KDP description — browse, search, and this book specifically

**Wendell, 2026-08-27:** *"dig into how we can take this specific book and align it for people who
are going to be browsing and searching as separate from the usual."*

**This is the analysis, not the description.** The copy itself goes through the full review pass
when it gets written, per the standing rule.

---

## 1 · The mechanics, only the parts that change a decision

| | |
|---|---|
| **Description field** | **4,000 characters, and HTML tags count against it.** Bold, italic, paragraph, break, headings and lists survive; CSS, inline styles and other attributes are stripped |
| **The fold** | Roughly the first **140–400 characters** show before *Read more* on mobile. The hook lives there or nowhere |
| **Backend keywords** | **7 fields × 50 characters**, and it is a **byte** limit — a smart quote or an em dash can cost 2+ bytes, and **overflowing a field can make Amazon ignore that whole field** |
| **Separators** | **Spaces, not commas.** Commas waste 10–15% of capacity for nothing |
| **Repeats** | Amazon concatenates all seven fields into one index. **A word needs to appear once, and never if it is already in the title** |

**The single most actionable line in all of that, for this book:** *allyship*, *game*, *mastering*
and *field guide* are **already in the title and subtitle**. The keyword fields are better spent
elsewhere, and the temptation to lead with them is strong precisely because they are the words the
book is about.

### Correction, 2026-08-27 — Wendell asked me to confirm this rather than trust it

**He was right to ask, and I had overstated it.** The first version of this line read *"they are
already indexed. **Every byte spent re-typing them in the keyword fields is wasted.**"* Stated as
mechanism. It is not mechanism.

**What actually holds up:**

- **Amazon's own guidance does advise against using information already covered in your metadata**,
  the title and contributors included. That part is real and it is Amazon's, not a blogger's.
- **The reasoning is efficiency, not indexing.** You do not *need* the slot, so it is better spent
  on a phrase the title does not already carry.
- **Authors are not penalised for repeating a title word.** It is a recommended practice rather
  than a rule, and nothing breaks if you ignore it.

**So the advice survives and the certainty does not.** *Better spent elsewhere* is supportable;
*every byte is wasted* implies a mechanical penalty that no source establishes.

**And I could not read the primary source.** `kdp.amazon.com` is blocked by this environment's
egress proxy, so this is Amazon's guidance **as reported by publishing-industry sources**, not as
read off Amazon's own help page. **Check the live page before committing the seven fields.**

## 2 · Browse and search are two different people arriving

**This is the part the usual advice flattens**, and it is what the question was actually asking.

| | the searcher | the browser |
|---|---|---|
| how they got here | typed a problem into a box | scrolling a category list |
| what they already know | their symptom | nothing about this book |
| what the first 140 characters must do | **confirm they are in the right place** | **differentiate against the twenty covers beside it** |
| what loses them | copy that describes a topic rather than their situation | copy that could be any of the twenty |

**The back cover's opening happens to serve both, for different reasons.** *"You started this work
for a reason. It's just gotten hard to hear that reason over all the noise of doing the work."*
For the searcher it is recognition — their symptom, said back. For the browser it is
differentiation — nineteen of the twenty books beside it open on the topic, and this one opens on
the reader.

**So the hook survives the port. Almost nothing after it should.**

## 3 · The vocabulary problem, which is this book's real KDP problem

**Nobody with this book's problem searches for *allyship*.**

The people who type *allyship* into Amazon are, in the main, two groups: readers already committed
to the topic as a topic, and **corporate buyers assembling a training list**. Neither is the ICA.
`IDEAL_READER_MATRIX` describes a Green reader whose live experience is exhaustion, not curiosity
about a category.

**What she actually types is the symptom.** Burnout from helping. Compassion fatigue. Why does
helping feel bad. How to help without losing myself. Boundaries with people I care about. Caring
too much. Helper burnout.

**That is the alignment the question is asking for.** The book is genuinely findable under
**burnout, boundaries and compassion-fatigue vocabulary** — those are what Chapter 1's token and
ticket systems and Chapter 7's Diplomat are *about* — and the description can carry that
vocabulary honestly, because the book delivers on it. **This is discovery rather than
keyword-stuffing**, and the distinction holds only because the claim is true.

**It also breaks a tie the book has been carrying since the cover.** *Allyship* is a contested
word that some readers approach and others avoid. **The symptom vocabulary is uncontested.**
Nobody has a political position on being tired.

**The two audiences want opposite descriptions, and that is a real cost.** A description tuned to
the exhausted helper will read as insufficiently serious to an L&D buyer scanning for a training
text. **The recommendation is to tune for the ICA and let the corporate buyer arrive through
`/speaking`**, which is built for them, priced for them, and already has the one-sheets. The book
page does not have to do every job.

## 4 · What changes when the back cover becomes an Amazon description

**Six things, and the first two are the ones that matter.**

1. **Four thousand characters instead of one hundred and sixty-eight.** The back cover was written
   to a physical ceiling. **The description has room for the specifics the back cover had to
   compress** — the six roles named, the four domains, the 120-card structure, what a reader
   walks out able to do. `ANALYSIS_SALES_PAGE` §D already made this argument for the Gumroad page:
   turn the contents list into outcomes, because *"the subject is what converts."*
2. **HTML.** Bold and lists are available, so the description can be **scannable**, which a back
   cover cannot be. A browser scanning twenty listings reads bolded fragments, not sentences.
3. **The disqualifier gets more expensive.** *"Not for you if you are looking to be told to care
   more"* is right on a back cover, where the reader is already holding the book. On Amazon the
   reader is lower-intent and cheaper to lose. **It should probably survive anyway** — the matrix
   says stacked-funnel energy is what loses this reader — **but it belongs at the end, below the
   fold, where it filters rather than deters.**
4. **No barcode, so no space competition with the bio.** The author bio can run longer here than
   on the jacket.
5. **The failure story earns more room.** *Under ten percent finished* is the single most
   arresting sentence available and on Amazon it can have its own line.
6. **Editorial reviews are a separate field.** Any blurb goes there, not in the description.

## 5 · Keywords, concretely

**Seven fields, spaces not commas, nothing already in the title.** Candidate groupings, to be
tested rather than trusted:

```
1  compassion fatigue helper burnout
2  boundaries for people who care too much
3  emotional labor exhaustion recovery
4  activist burnout sustainable practice
5  how to help without burning out
6  self awareness shadow work practice
7  workplace inclusion practical guide
```

**Every one of those is a phrase a person types**, which is the only test that matters. Long-tail
phrases beat single words because they carry intent, and intent is what converts.

**Categories are a separate lever and possibly the stronger one.** Amazon has 16,000+ of them and
they are how browsers travel. **The book has two plausible homes and they lead to different
readers** — the DEI/social-change shelf, where the corporate buyer browses, and the
self-help/burnout shelf, where the ICA browses. **Pick for the ICA**, for the same reason as §3,
and accept a smaller, winnable list over a larger one nobody in your audience walks down.

## 5a · Doing the keyword research for free

**Wendell, 2026-08-27:** *"lets do research on how people are using AI for free on this. Look for
open source tooling or any sort of free guide."*

**The finding that makes this cheap: most paid tools are a wrapper around a free endpoint.** They
query **Amazon's own autocomplete**, present the results in a nicer interface, and charge for the
presentation. The endpoint is the same one the search box uses.

| approach | cost | what it gives |
|---|---|---|
| **Amazon's search box, directly** | free | Type a seed phrase and read the dropdown. It is the actual demand signal, and it is the input the paid tools resell |
| **The alphabet-soup method** | free | Type the seed, then a space, then each letter in turn — *helper burnout a*, *helper burnout b* — and harvest what Amazon completes. Indie authors have run this since about 2015 |
| **`KDP Scout`** | free, open source | A command-line tool that mines Amazon autocomplete and expands seed phrases into hundreds of long-tail variants. **The same data, without the markup** |
| **AMZ Suggestion Expander** | free Chrome extension | Surfaces the expanded dropdown inline while you search |
| **Publisher Rocket** | ~$97 one-off | Adds estimated competition and volume figures on top of the same autocomplete base |

**On using an LLM for this, including me.** Dave Chesson's objection is the correct one and it
applies directly to §5 of this document: **a language model has no Amazon search-volume data**, so
when it proposes phrases it is pattern-matching rather than measuring. **That is exactly what the
seven candidate groupings in §5 are** — ideation, generated from the ICA, and worth nothing until
Amazon's autocomplete confirms a real person types them.

**So the honest division of labour is: the model proposes, the autocomplete disposes.** Take the
seven groupings, type each into Amazon's search box, and keep only what the dropdown actually
completes. **That costs an hour and no money**, and it converts every phrase in this document from
inference into evidence.

**Buy Publisher Rocket only if competition numbers change a decision.** For seven fields on one
book, the free path reaches the same place.

## 6 · What I could not check, and it is a real gap

- **Search volume.** I have no keyword tool and Amazon is not reachable from this session. **The
  seven groupings above are reasoned from the ICA, not measured.** Publisher Rocket is the
  standard instrument for this and it would replace inference with numbers — worth the cost before
  committing the seven fields.
- **The live competitive set.** What actually ranks for *allyship* and for the symptom phrases,
  and how the twenty covers beside this one open, is a browse away and I cannot make it.
- **The current category slot count.** KDP has changed this more than once; check what the
  dashboard offers today rather than trusting a number from an article.

## 7 · The order I would work it

1. **Settle the two audiences.** Tune for the ICA, route the corporate buyer to `/speaking`. This
   decides everything below it.
2. **Run one keyword tool** against the symptom vocabulary before writing, so the description's
   nouns are the ones people type.
3. **Draft the description** — hook ported from the back cover, then the outcomes, then the
   failure story, then the disqualifier below the fold. **Full review pass**, since it is
   customer-facing.
4. **Pick categories last**, once the description has made the book's promise sharp — category
   strategy works better on a sharp promise than a broad one.
