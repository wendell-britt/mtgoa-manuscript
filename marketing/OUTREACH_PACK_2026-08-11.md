## 3 · ACCT — Call for Presenters *(open now)*

**Conference:** 37th Annual International Conference, Jan 20–24 2027, Marriott Tacoma Downtown
**Audience to write for:** practitioners, facilitators, trainers — not builders or inspectors

**Title**

**The Participant Who Won't Go: Working Fear Instead of Around It**

**Session description**

There is a moment every facilitator knows. Someone is at the base of the element, the group is
watching, and the thing in front of you is not a technical problem. Most of our training tells
us what to say. Very little of it tells us what is actually happening in that person, or in us.

This session works a specific model: five channels — fear, sadness, joy, anger, neutrality —
each of which is telling you something different about what a participant needs next. Fear that
gets managed rather than metabolized produces two predictable outcomes, and facilitators will
recognize both immediately: the participant who goes and shouldn't have, and the participant who
quietly checks out for the rest of the day.

We'll run the read live, on real scenarios from the room. You'll leave with a script for the
ninety seconds at the base of the element, and a way to debrief it afterward that doesn't
require the participant to perform a breakthrough they didn't have.

I built a course on this material in 2020. Under ten percent of the people who started it
finished, and rebuilding from that failure is where the method actually came from. I'll open
with that.

**Learning objectives**

1. **Identify** which of five emotional channels is running in a hesitating participant, from
   observable behavior rather than self-report.
2. **Apply** a ninety-second intervention at the base of an element that works the fear rather
   than routing around it.
3. **Redesign** one debrief prompt they currently use so it stops requiring participants to
   perform a resolution.

**Format:** workshop, interactive, 60–90 min · **Level:** all facilitators

## 5 · The forwardable blurb — for update #2

**The design decision that makes this work:** it's written **in the backer's voice, not yours.**
The usual mistake is shipping a paragraph about yourself, which reads as a press release the
moment someone pastes it into an email. This reads like a person recommending something, because
that's what it has to survive as.

### General

I backed a book called *Mastering the Game of Allyship* — it came out this year and it's the
first thing I've read on the subject that's about what to actually do rather than what to
believe. The author, Wendell Britt, speaks and runs workshops on it; the short version of his
pitch is that most training on this doesn't stick, and he can prove it, because his own course
had under a ten percent completion rate before he rebuilt the method around that failure. He's
at <wendell@masteringallyship.com>, and I've attached the whole book so you can judge it
yourself rather than take my word for it.

### For camps, outdoor programs, and field staff

I backed a book this year called *Mastering the Game of Allyship*, and the part that stuck with
me was its model of how people fail under pressure — anger and fear that stay stuck produce
recklessness or resignation, which is basically a description of every incident report I've ever
read. The author, Wendell Britt, does staff training on it; he's presented at the Wilderness Risk
Management Conference and been paid by Oregon Outdoor School, and he prices per seat rather than
as a speaking fee. Book's attached — his stuff on why training doesn't survive the season is
worth the ten minutes even if you never book him.

### For HR, L&D, and ERG leads

I backed a book called *Mastering the Game of Allyship* that's genuinely different from the
usual — it treats this as a set of learnable moves instead of a values statement, and it's aimed
at the continuity problem specifically: why programs die when the person running them moves on.
The author, Wendell Britt, ran alumni relations and employee engagement before writing it, so
